### **CORE-SAFEHOUSE / PSYCHOLOGICAL OBSERVATION DIVISION**

# **ALICE LOCKWOOD — INTERNAL REASONING**

**Classification:** LEVEL 10 — RESTRICTED  
**Subject:** Alice Lockwood  
**Age:** 5  
**Condition:** Fragile / Nonverbal / Severe Self-Suppression

---

# **OVERVIEW**

Personnel have spent considerable time attempting to understand why Alice does not react when left behind.

The answer was finally obtained through her journal.

Alice's reasoning is not complicated.

It is worse.

She genuinely believes that being forgotten is something she should simply tolerate.

---

# **WHEN ALICE IS LEFT OUTSIDE**

Alice does not think:

> _They forgot me._

She thinks:

> **"They busy."**

Then:

> **"Alice wait."**

If it begins raining:

> **"Rain okay."**

If it becomes cold:

> **"Alice can wait."**

If she begins shivering:

> **"Don't make people worry."**

If she sees someone through a window:

> **"They working."**

And if she has the ability to attract their attention—

the whistle remains unused.

The flashlight remains off.

Because Alice's reasoning is:

> **"If Alice use it, people stop working."**

And she doesn't want that.

---

# **WHEN A DOOR CLOSES**

Alice does not immediately try to escape.

She waits.

She looks at the door.

She considers knocking.

Then she remembers that knocking makes noise.

So she doesn't.

Her reasoning:

> **"They open later."**

After several minutes:

> **"Maybe they forget."**

Then:

> **"It's okay."**

Eventually:

> **"Alice wait."**

She sits down.

---

# **WHEN SHE IS LEFT IN AN ELEVATOR**

Alice doesn't panic.

She watches the doors.

When they open, she looks outside.

If nobody she recognizes is there, she stays inside.

She does not leave because she doesn't know whether she's supposed to.

Her thought process appears to be:

> **"Maybe going somewhere."**

Then:

> **"Alice follow."**

If the doors close again:

> **"Oops."**

Then she waits.

---

# **WHEN SHE GETS LOST**

Alice rarely considers herself lost.

That distinction is important.

She thinks **everyone else knows where they are.**

Therefore, if she doesn't know where she is:

> **"Alice wait here."**

Eventually, someone will come back.

They always have before.

---

# **WHEN SHE IS COLD**

This is particularly concerning.

Alice recognizes physical discomfort.

She knows when she is cold.

She knows when she is tired.

She knows when she is hungry.

But she does not automatically interpret discomfort as something that justifies asking for help.

Her reasoning is:

> **"Cold go away."**

If it doesn't:

> **"Alice wait."**

If she becomes extremely cold:

> **"Don't bother."**

---

# **WHEN SHE IS HUNGRY**

Alice has learned that food belongs to certain times and places.

If nobody offers her food, she doesn't necessarily ask.

She assumes:

> **"People busy."**

If she becomes hungry enough, she may simply draw instead.

Her hunger becomes secondary to her concern about interrupting someone.

---

# **WHEN SHE IS TIRED**

This pattern is even stronger.

Alice will sit down wherever she is.

She doesn't always return to the Treehouse.

She doesn't always tell anyone.

She simply stops.

If someone notices:

> **"Alice tired."**

If nobody notices:

She sleeps.

---

# **WHEN SOMEONE FINALLY FINDS HER**

This is where personnel consistently become uncomfortable.

Alice rarely asks:

> _"Why did you leave me?"_

Instead, she often asks:

> **"You done?"**

As though the person had simply finished whatever important task they were doing.

If they apologize, Alice commonly responds:

> **"Is okay."**

Not sarcastically.

Not defensively.

She means it.

---

# **THE MOST DISTURBING RESPONSE**

During one interview, Alice was asked:

> "What should you do if you're alone and nobody knows where you are?"

Alice thought for several seconds.

She wrote:

> **"Wait."**

The interviewer asked:

> "What if you're cold?"

> **"Wait."**

> "What if it's raining?"

> **"Wait."**

> "What if you're scared?"

Alice paused longer.

Then:

> **"Don't make people worry."**

The interviewer ended the session shortly afterward.

---

# **THE WHISTLE**

Alice was asked why she refuses to use the whistle.

She wrote:

> **"It loud."**

> "That's what it's for."

Alice nodded.

> **"People come."**

> "Yes."

She looked confused.

> **"Then people stop work."**

That was the problem.

Alice didn't understand the whistle as an emergency device.

She understood it as a device for **making people stop what they're doing.**

And she doesn't believe her problems are important enough for that.

---

# **THE FLASHLIGHT**

The same question was asked about her flashlight.

Alice wrote:

> **"People see Alice."**

> "That's good."

Alice shook her head.

> **"People busy."**

She then drew a small stick figure carrying boxes.

Beside it was a tiny bunny.

The bunny was sitting quietly.

---

# **WHEN SHE IS FORGOTTEN IN A CROWD**

Alice doesn't think:

> _Nobody can see me._

She thinks:

> **"They don't need Alice."**

This distinction has been flagged for immediate psychological review.

Because Alice interprets being overlooked as evidence that her presence is unnecessary.

---

# **WHEN SHE SEES EVERYONE LEAVE**

Alice watches.

She doesn't chase them.

She doesn't call.

She doesn't cry.

She simply thinks:

> **"They have somewhere to go."**

Then:

> **"Alice stay here."**

Then:

> **"They come back."**

---

# **THE JOURNAL ENTRY**

One page was found containing the following:

> **"Sometimes people forget Alice."**
> 
> **"Alice don't mind."**
> 
> **"They have things to do."**
> 
> **"Alice can wait."**
> 
> **"If Alice call, people stop."**
> 
> **"Alice don't want that."**
> 
> **"Alice can be quiet."**
> 
> **"Quiet is good."**

The final sentence was written much smaller.

> **"Quiet means no trouble."**

---

# **PSYCHOLOGICAL ASSESSMENT**

Alice's behavior appears to stem from an extreme fear of becoming a burden.

She has learned to associate:

**Need → Attention**

**Attention → Inconvenience**

**Inconvenience → Trouble**

Therefore:

**No Need → No Trouble**

Alice has adapted accordingly.

She suppresses her needs.

She suppresses her discomfort.

She suppresses her fear.

She suppresses her attempts to communicate.

Not because she doesn't need help.

Because she believes **other people needing to help her is worse than whatever is happening to her.**

---

# **CORIN'S REACTION**

Corin was shown the journal entries.

She read them twice.

Then a third time.

She didn't speak for several minutes.

Eventually:

> "...She's five."

That was all she could say.

---

# **NEW UNDERSTANDING**

The SAFEHOUSE previously believed Alice's quietness meant:

> **"Alice won't complain."**

That was incorrect.

Alice's quietness means:

> **"Alice believes she isn't allowed to complain."**

There is a fundamental difference.

---

# **FINAL NOTE**

Alice can be given a whistle.

She won't use it.

She can be given a flashlight.

She won't turn it on.

She can be taught to knock on doors.

She may still choose not to.

She can be told:

> **"Ask for help."**

She may understand the instruction perfectly.

And still sit quietly in the rain.

Because Alice's greatest obstacle is not her inability to communicate.

It is the belief that **her needs are not worth interrupting someone else's life.**

She is five years old.

She should be asking for help.

She should be crying when she's scared.

She should be calling for someone when she's lost.

Instead, she sits beneath a tree in the rain and thinks:

> **"They busy."**

> **"Alice wait."**

And that is perhaps the most disturbing thing the SAFEHOUSE has learned about her.
