# SAFEHOUSE PSYCHOLOGICAL FOLLOW-UP

**Classification:** CONFIDENTIAL — RESIDENT WELFARE  
**Subject:** Neri  
**Related Subject:** Alice  
**Time Since Last Confirmed Sighting:** Approximately 3 Months  
**Current Assessment:** Persistent Complicated Grief / Survivor Guilt  
**Alice Status:** MISSING  
**Neri's Belief:** Alice was lost because of her

---

## 1. Current State

Three months have passed since Neri came to believe that Alice may have been lost because of her.

Alice remains missing.

No definitive evidence of death has been recovered.

No confirmed communication has been received.

Neri nevertheless appears to have internally moved from:

> **“Alice is missing.”**

to:

> **“I lost Alice.”**

That distinction is now deeply embedded in her thinking.

---

# 2. Neri's Interpretation

Neri does not generally say:

> “Alice might be dead.”

She increasingly says:

> **“I lost her.”**

When asked what she means, she occasionally clarifies:

> “I was the last person.”

She considers the conversation between herself and Alice to be a personal failure.

She remembers misunderstanding Alice.

She remembers leaving.

She remembers Alice disappearing afterward.

She remembers learning about Alice's previous history of running away.

And she remembers that Alice's greatest fear was abandonment.

Neri has consequently constructed a simple explanation from an extremely complicated series of events:

> **Alice was afraid of being abandoned.  
> I walked away.  
> Alice disappeared.  
> Therefore, I abandoned her.**

This conclusion is emotionally compelling to Neri.

It is not medically or evidentially established.

---

# 3. Why This Is Different For Neri

For another child, losing a friend could be devastating.

For Neri, it is almost a reenactment of her entire childhood.

She has already experienced:

- family members treating people as disposable,
    
- the loss of her younger sister,
    
- helplessness during her sister's death,
    
- repeated loss of friends,
    
- prolonged isolation,
    
- captivity,
    
- experimentation,
    
- and the belief that nobody would come for her.
    

Neri's deepest fear has never simply been:

> **“Someone might die.”**

It has been:

> **“Someone will disappear, and I will be unable to stop it.”**

Alice's disappearance fits that fear almost perfectly.

---

# 4. The Particularly Cruel Part

Neri now knows Alice hated abandonment.

She also knows Alice had repeatedly run away herself.

She knows Alice had experienced memory loss.

She knows Alice could become isolated and neglect herself.

She knows Alice had previously disappeared from the people who cared about her.

And yet Neri's mind keeps returning to one moment:

> **She walked away.**

Neri has therefore assigned herself responsibility for something far larger than one conversation.

She does not appear to recognize the difference between:

**“I regret what I did.”**

and

**“I caused what happened.”**

To her, they have become the same statement.

---

# 5. Grief Without Confirmation

The absence of a body or confirmed death creates an additional complication.

Neri cannot properly say goodbye.

She cannot mourn Alice as definitively dead.

She cannot comfortably hope Alice is alive.

She exists in between.

Every unexpected sound outside the SAFEHOUSE can theoretically be Alice.

Every time the door opens, Neri sometimes looks up.

Every unfamiliar figure at a distance briefly becomes a possibility.

Then reality returns.

This has prevented the grief from reaching any conventional conclusion.

---

# 6. Changes in Neri

Neri continues to isolate herself.

The cafeteria remains one of her most common locations late at night.

However, cake is no longer consistently present.

She still loves sweets.

She still eats them.

But she no longer seems to use them as reliably as she once did for comfort.

Her relationship with her friends has also become more complicated.

She remains deeply attached to Bao and Frost.

She continues to trust them.

But she occasionally becomes noticeably anxious when someone leaves without telling her where they are going.

She has begun asking questions such as:

> “You're coming back, right?”

or:

> “How long?”

These questions should not be dismissed as ordinary childhood behavior.

---

# 7. Medical Interaction

Neri's worsening anemia has made the situation more dangerous.

She can become critically ill without warning.

Her increasing isolation creates opportunities for an episode to go unnoticed.

This creates an unfortunate feedback loop:

**Grief → isolation → increased medical risk → increased concern from staff → Neri retreats further.**

Personnel must interrupt this cycle without making Neri feel imprisoned.

---

# 8. What Neri Needs to Hear

Personnel should not repeatedly tell Neri:

> “It isn't your fault.”

Although factually appropriate as a general reassurance, Neri currently interprets it as an attempt to dismiss her feelings.

Instead:

> “You wish you had done something differently.”

> “You miss her.”

> “You regret walking away.”

> “None of those things mean you controlled what happened to Alice.”

The goal is not to remove Neri's grief.

The goal is to separate grief from self-condemnation.

---

# 9. A Difficult Truth

Neri may genuinely have made a mistake.

She misunderstood Alice.

She left a conversation.

She hurt someone unintentionally.

She wishes she had behaved differently.

Those things can all be true.

And yet:

**Alice's disappearance remains Alice's own history, choices, circumstances, and unknown whereabouts.**

Neri cannot reasonably be assigned responsibility for all of them.

A ten-year-old cannot be made responsible for another person's entire history of trauma.

---

# 10. Current Psychological Position

Neri's previous worldview was:

> **“People disappear because nobody cares.”**

SAFEHOUSE attempted to replace it with:

> **“People stay. People come looking. You matter.”**

Alice's disappearance has complicated that progress.

Neri now appears to believe:

> **“People disappear even when they care.”**

And beneath that:

> **“Sometimes I am the reason they disappear.”**

This belief requires careful intervention.

---

# 11. Archivist's Observation

During one evening observation, Neri was sitting alone in the cafeteria.

An Archivist asked:

> “Do you miss her?”

Neri answered immediately:

> “Yes.”

The Archivist asked:

> “Do you think she's dead?”

Neri stared at the table.

> “I don't know.”

A pause.

> “I think I lost her.”

The Archivist asked:

> “Do you want to find her?”

Neri's answer came quietly:

> **“More than anything.”**

She then looked toward the cafeteria entrance.

> “But what if she doesn't want me anymore?”

No answer was recorded.

---

# 12. Final Assessment

Neri's grief is complicated because **Alice is both absent and potentially alive.**

Neri is grieving a friendship that she believes she destroyed.

She is mourning someone whose death has not been confirmed.

She is carrying guilt for an outcome she cannot prove she caused.

And all of this is occurring within a child whose previous experiences taught her that losing someone means she failed to protect them.

It would be incorrect to tell Neri:

> “You lost Alice.”

It would be equally incorrect to tell her:

> “You had nothing to do with it.”

The truth is more uncomfortable:

> **We don't know what happened to Alice.**

Neri cannot know either.

---

## FINAL ENTRY

Three months have passed.

Neri still believes she lost a friend with her own hands.

Not because she wanted Alice gone.

Not because she hated her.

Not because she stopped caring.

But because she finally understands what Alice was trying to say—

**and she understood it after walking away.**

For Neri, that timing is almost unbearable.

She has spent her life believing that when someone disappears, she should have done something.

Now she has found someone she believes she could have saved.

Whether she actually could have is unknown.

That uncertainty is the part she cannot forgive herself for.
