## I. FINAL DETERMINATION

**Tom Akana cannot be brought back.**

The conclusion has been confirmed beyond the scope of any remaining recovery procedure available to C.O.R.E.

Genesis Construction cannot restore him.

Jake Akana cannot reverse the event.

The Architects cannot intervene to recover him.

No known anomalous mechanism, external intervention, or reconstruction process can return Tom to existence.

This is not a temporary demanifestation.

It is not a manifestation awaiting the correct stimulus.

It is not an inaccessible location, a dormant state, or a problem that can be solved by discovering the right sequence of actions.

Tom is gone.

The previous designation of _MISSING — PERMANENT DEMANIFESTATION SUSPECTED; RECOVERY UNKNOWN_ is hereby retired.

His status is now recorded as:

**TOM AKANA — IRRECOVERABLE EXISTENTIAL LOSS.**

The circumstances of the initial incident remain accidental. Jake did not intend to permanently demanifest Tom. That distinction does not alter the outcome, but it remains essential to the assessment of Jake's responsibility and psychological welfare.

C.O.R.E. will not authorize further recovery experiments.

No personnel are to encourage Jake to keep trying.

No one is to suggest that the Architects have withheld a solution, that sufficient effort might reverse the event, or that Tom is waiting somewhere beyond their reach.

The possibility of recovery is no longer to be used as reassurance.

The investigation is closed with respect to restoration.

The grief is not.

## II. THE LAST ATTEMPT

The final confirmation was delivered to Jake in a private room.

The meeting was attended by authorized C.O.R.E. personnel and a residential welfare specialist. No attempt was made to surround Jake with a large group of residents.

He had already heard too many people tell him that they were looking into it.

He had already asked too many times whether the next attempt might work.

The following transcript was recorded by the attending welfare specialist.

**C.O.R.E. REPRESENTATIVE:** "Jake, we have the final results."

Jake was sitting on the edge of the bed.

He did not look up.

**JAKE:** "Did you find him?"

The representative paused.

**C.O.R.E. REPRESENTATIVE:** "No."

Jake's fingers tightened around the fabric beneath him.

**JAKE:** "What about the Architects?"

**C.O.R.E. REPRESENTATIVE:** "They cannot bring him back."

**JAKE:** "But they can do things that we can't."

**C.O.R.E. REPRESENTATIVE:** "I know."

**JAKE:** "They can fix things."

**C.O.R.E. REPRESENTATIVE:** "They cannot fix this."

Jake finally looked up.

**JAKE:** "...What about me?"

No one answered immediately.

**JAKE:** "He's my creation. I made him. I should be able to undo it."

**C.O.R.E. REPRESENTATIVE:** "We have exhausted the available methods. You cannot restore Tom."

**JAKE:** "Then find another method."

**C.O.R.E. REPRESENTATIVE:** "Jake—"

**JAKE:** "FIND ONE!"

His voice cracked.

**JAKE:** "I can make things! I can bring things into the world! I can make people! Why can't I make him come back?!"

The representative allowed him to finish.

**JAKE:** "He's supposed to be here!"

His eyes filled with tears.

**JAKE:** "He always comes back."

The room became quiet.

Jake looked toward the doorway.

For a moment, he seemed to expect Tom to appear there, wearing the same familiar expression of weary disbelief he used whenever Jake did something reckless.

Nothing happened.

**JAKE:** "...You're lying."

**C.O.R.E. REPRESENTATIVE:** "I'm sorry."

**JAKE:** "You're lying."

**C.O.R.E. REPRESENTATIVE:** "I wish I were."

Jake stared at the floor.

His shoulders began to shake.

**JAKE:** "I didn't mean to."

**C.O.R.E. REPRESENTATIVE:** "I know."

**JAKE:** "I didn't want him to go."

**C.O.R.E. REPRESENTATIVE:** "I know."

**JAKE:** "Then why can't you bring him back?"

There was no answer that could make the truth less cruel.

The representative moved closer but did not touch Jake without permission.

**C.O.R.E. REPRESENTATIVE:** "You don't have to decide what to do with all of this today."

Jake shook his head.

**JAKE:** "I don't want all of this."

He wiped his face with his sleeve.

**JAKE:** "I just want Tom."

The meeting ended without further questions.

Jake did not attempt Genesis Construction again that evening.

He did not ask for cake.

He did not ask anyone to play.

He went to his room and closed the door.

For the first time since the incident, he stopped trying to bring Tom back.

Not because he had accepted the loss.

Because he had finally been told there was nothing left to try.

## III. THE DAYS AFTERWARD

The residents initially believed that the worst part of the crisis had passed.

The investigation was over. There would be no more failed experiments, no more waiting for the next report, and no more speculation about whether an Architect might discover an exception.

They expected grief.

They did not understand what grief would look like in Jake.

### Day One

Jake remained in his room for most of the day.

When a resident knocked, he answered.

When asked whether he wanted company, he said no.

When asked whether he needed anything, he said no again.

The residents respected his request for space, but the welfare team maintained appropriate contact because of his recent history of suicidal behavior.

At dinner, Jake came downstairs.

He sat at the table and watched the others eat.

Someone asked whether he wanted anything.

**JAKE:** "No, thank you."

He returned upstairs without eating.

### Day Two

Jake attempted to play one of the games he and Tom used to play together.

He stopped before finishing the setup.

A resident found him sitting beside the unfinished game.

**RESIDENT:** "Do you want me to play with you?"

**JAKE:** "No."

**RESIDENT:** "Are you sure?"

Jake stared at the pieces.

**JAKE:** "It's not the same."

The resident sat down nearby.

**RESIDENT:** "No. It isn't."

Jake did not ask the resident to leave.

He did not ask them to stay, either.

### Day Three

Jake stopped referring to Tom in the present tense.

He no longer said, _"Tom will be back."_

He began saying, _"Tom would've liked this."_

The distinction was noticed by the welfare team.

It was not interpreted as evidence that Jake had accepted the loss.

Sometimes, people stop expecting a person to return because they have begun to understand the permanence of the loss.

Sometimes, they stop because continuing to hope hurts too much.

C.O.R.E. could not determine which explanation applied.

### Day Four

Jake asked whether Tom's room would be left alone.

The answer was yes.

He asked whether anyone would move Tom's belongings.

The answer was no.

He asked whether the residents would eventually stop talking about Tom.

The welfare specialist answered honestly.

**WELFARE SPECIALIST:** "I can't promise that everyone will remember him in exactly the same way. But we won't pretend he never existed."

Jake nodded.

**JAKE:** "Okay."

He went upstairs.

The following day, he asked the same question again.

No one criticized him for repeating it.

---

## IV. THE SECOND PHASE — WHEN GRIEF BECAME QUIET

The initial days were marked by visible distress.

Jake cried. He shouted. He tried to use Genesis Construction. He demanded answers that nobody could give him.

After the final determination, those behaviors diminished.

At first, some residents interpreted the change as improvement.

Jake was quieter.

He stopped demanding that the Architects intervene.

He no longer attempted to reverse Tom's demanifestation.

He began answering questions with complete sentences again.

He occasionally joined the residents for meals.

But his quietness concealed a different development.

Jake had begun to behave as though there was no point in asking anyone to stay.

When a resident was busy, he no longer asked whether they could play later.

When someone said they had to leave, he did not ask when they would return.

When offered companionship, he sometimes accepted, but rarely initiated it.

The change was not consistent. There were moments when he laughed, became excited about a game, or spoke enthusiastically about something he had created.

Those moments were real.

They did not prove that he was recovering.

During one welfare conversation, the specialist asked him why he had stopped asking people to play.

**WELFARE SPECIALIST:** "You used to ask everyone."

**JAKE:** "I know."

**WELFARE SPECIALIST:** "Do you still want to play?"

**JAKE:** "Sometimes."

**WELFARE SPECIALIST:** "Then why don't you ask?"

Jake thought about the question.

**JAKE:** "They can say no."

**WELFARE SPECIALIST:** "Yes. They can."

**JAKE:** "And they have things to do."

**WELFARE SPECIALIST:** "They do."

Jake looked down.

**JAKE:** "So I shouldn't bother them."

**WELFARE SPECIALIST:** "Asking someone to spend time with you isn't bothering them."

**JAKE:** "It feels like it."

The specialist did not attempt to argue the feeling away.

**WELFARE SPECIALIST:** "I'm sorry it feels that way."

Jake remained quiet.

Then he said something that was added to the case record.

**JAKE:** "Tom was busy sometimes, too."

**WELFARE SPECIALIST:** "Yes."

**JAKE:** "But he came back."

A long pause followed.

**JAKE:** "Now he can't."

The conversation ended there.

The specialist's assessment noted that Jake appeared to be connecting ordinary absences with the permanent loss of Tom. The loss had not merely removed one source of companionship. It had altered the meaning Jake attached to companionship itself.

Previously, someone leaving the room could be endured because Tom would return.

Now Jake had experienced a loss that no one could reverse.

He could no longer rely on the assumption that everyone who disappeared would eventually come back.

C.O.R.E. could not promise him otherwise.

It could only help him understand that Tom's loss did not mean every relationship would end the same way—and that asking for companionship was not an act of foolishness simply because no one could promise permanence.

## V. SUBSEQUENT INCIDENTS — DEATH WITHOUT WITNESSES

**Classification:** Restricted medical addendum  
**Period covered:** Subsequent weeks following Tom Akana's confirmed irrecoverable loss

Jake died more often in the period that followed.

The incidents were not identical, and C.O.R.E. does not claim a single confirmed cause for every occurrence. However, the timing, the observed changes in Jake's behavior, and his previous history indicate a continuing and serious risk of self-harm.

A particularly concerning pattern emerged.

**Jake began dying alone.**

He did not always seek help before a crisis.

He did not always alert nearby residents.

On some occasions, no one realized what had happened until a welfare check or an unexpected absence brought them to his room.

His resurrection mechanism continued to function inconsistently. In some incidents, white fire manifested after a delay. In others, the interval was difficult to establish because no one had witnessed the onset of death.

The medical team recorded each event separately.

No incident was to be dismissed as routine.

No staff member was permitted to assume that Jake would return simply because he had returned before.

The following interview was conducted after one of the later incidents, once Jake was medically stable and able to participate.

**WELFARE SPECIALIST:** "Can you tell me what was happening before the incident?"

Jake stared at the table.

**JAKE:** "I don't remember all of it."

**WELFARE SPECIALIST:** "That's okay. We don't have to force it."

A pause.

**JAKE:** "I didn't want anyone to find me."

The specialist remained still.

**WELFARE SPECIALIST:** "Can you tell me why?"

Jake's fingers curled into his sleeves.

**JAKE:** "They get scared."

**WELFARE SPECIALIST:** "You're worried about frightening them?"

**JAKE:** "Tom got scared."

The specialist did not interrupt.

**JAKE:** "Every time I died, he got scared. Even when I came back."

His voice grew quieter.

**JAKE:** "He was always scared."

**WELFARE SPECIALIST:** "He loved you."

**JAKE:** "I know."

**WELFARE SPECIALIST:** "And it hurt him to see you suffer."

Jake nodded.

**JAKE:** "I don't want anyone else to have to do that."

The specialist paused before responding.

**WELFARE SPECIALIST:** "I understand why you don't want people to be frightened. But dying alone doesn't protect them from losing you. It can put you in more danger, especially when your resurrection isn't predictable."

Jake did not answer.

**WELFARE SPECIALIST:** "You don't have to protect us from knowing you're hurting."

**JAKE:** "I don't know how to tell anyone."

**WELFARE SPECIALIST:** "Then we can start with something small. You don't have to explain everything. You can tell someone, 'I'm not safe by myself right now.'"

Jake looked away.

**JAKE:** "What if they don't come?"

The specialist took a breath.

**WELFARE SPECIALIST:** "Then our job is to make sure there is another person to contact, and another way to get help. We cannot promise that one particular person will always be available. We can build a system so that you aren't left with only one person to turn to."

Jake was silent for a long time.

**JAKE:** "Tom was supposed to be that person."

The specialist did not contradict him.

**WELFARE SPECIALIST:** "Yes. He was important to you."

Jake's eyes filled with tears.

**JAKE:** "I made him disappear."

**WELFARE SPECIALIST:** "You accidentally caused the demanifestation. That is not the same as choosing to lose him."

**JAKE:** "He's still gone."

**WELFARE SPECIALIST:** "Yes."

The specialist allowed the truth to remain in the room.

**WELFARE SPECIALIST:** "And you are still here. We need to take care of you, too."

Jake cried quietly.

The interview concluded with a revised safety plan and increased residential support.

The report did not record a promise from Jake that he would never die again.

C.O.R.E. would not treat such a promise as a reliable safeguard.

The goal was to make it easier for Jake to seek help before a crisis became fatal, not to make him responsible for guaranteeing that he would never struggle again.

## VI. RESIDENTIAL REVIEW — THE PEOPLE WHO COULD NOT REPLACE TOM

Several residents attempted to spend more time with Jake after Tom's loss.

Their intentions were compassionate.

Some invited him to meals. Others offered games, quiet company, or help with Genesis Construction projects.

Jake accepted some invitations and declined others.

At first, the residents worried that they were failing him.

They were not Tom.

They did not share Tom's memories.

They could not reproduce the exact rhythm of their arguments, the private jokes, or the familiarity that had developed over years of companionship.

Some residents attempted to reassure Jake by emphasizing how many people still loved him.

The welfare team discouraged using that fact to argue against his grief.

During one exchange, a resident told Jake that he still had a whole family in SAFEHOUSE.

Jake answered:

**JAKE:** "I know."

**RESIDENT:** "You aren't alone."

Jake looked at the empty chair beside him.

**JAKE:** "I know."

The resident appeared confused.

**RESIDENT:** "Then why do you keep looking over there?"

Jake did not answer.

The resident later apologized for asking the question.

The incident illustrates an important distinction.

Jake can know that other people are physically present and still experience the absence of Tom as an overwhelming loss.

The existence of other relationships does not cancel a specific relationship.

He is not required to love the others less, or Tom less, to make room for grief.

Nor must the residents compete with Tom's memory.

They must allow the relationship to remain what it was.

Tom was not a placeholder for companionship.

He was Tom.

No amount of social activity can make that fact untrue.

## VII. C.O.R.E. DIRECTIVES — PERMANENT LOSS, CONTINUING CARE

### Directive 01: The Search for Tom Is Closed

No further recovery experiments are authorized.

Tom's absence is not to be used as an incentive for Jake to demonstrate greater control over Genesis Construction.

The incident will remain documented, and any genuinely new evidence may be reviewed through formal channels. However, personnel are not to create false hope by reopening procedures that have already been determined incapable of restoring him.

### Directive 02: Jake's Deaths Require Individual Emergency Responses

Each new incident must be treated as a distinct medical emergency.

His resurrection ability is not a guarantee of survival, and previous successful revivals do not establish that the next one will occur.

No one is to wait for white fire before seeking assistance.

No one is to assume that Jake's silence means he wishes to be left alone.

When his immediate safety is in question, the residential team must act.

### Directive 03: Reduce Isolation Without Making Companionship Mandatory

Jake will receive a predictable, shared support schedule with several trusted residents and designated staff.

The schedule must provide clear options for contact when his preferred person is unavailable.

It must not turn companionship into a forced performance or make Jake feel that every conversation is a clinical evaluation.

He is entitled to quiet time.

He is not to be left without access to help when quiet time becomes dangerous.

### Directive 04: Do Not Use Tom's Memory as Leverage

Personnel must not tell Jake that Tom would be disappointed in him, that he should stay alive for Tom, or that further incidents dishonor Tom's memory.

Such statements risk turning grief into another source of shame.

Tom's love is not to be used as a weapon against the person he loved.

### Directive 05: Preserve Tom's Place in SAFEHOUSE History

Tom's name, belongings, and records are to be preserved according to residential policy.

No one is to erase his existence to make Jake's recovery appear simpler.

No one is to insist that Jake keep his room unchanged forever, either. Decisions about the space must be made with sensitivity to his wishes and without imposing a deadline for grief.

### Directive 06: Provide Continuing Psychological Care

Jake's recent history includes prolonged isolation, repeated suicidal crises, an irrecoverable loss caused by an anomalous accident, and subsequent deaths occurring alone.

These circumstances require continuing professional support.

Care must be trauma-informed, developmentally appropriate, and responsive to Jake's own account of his experience.

Progress is not to be measured solely by how often he smiles, how much he eats, or how long he goes without mentioning Tom.

Silence is not proof of healing.

Laughter is not proof that grief has ended.

A difficult day is not proof that every previous effort has failed.

The objective is to help Jake remain alive, reconnect with others at his own pace, and learn to seek support without believing that his needs make him a burden.

## VIII. FINAL RESIDENTIAL OBSERVATION

A member of the welfare team recorded one final exchange after Jake had returned from a later medical observation.

The exchange occurred in the seventh-floor common area.

Jake had been sitting alone for approximately twenty minutes.

A resident approached and asked whether he wanted company.

**JAKE:** "You can sit if you want."

The resident sat across from him.

Neither spoke for several minutes.

Eventually, the resident asked:

**RESIDENT:** "Do you miss him?"

Jake looked at them as if the question were unnecessary.

**JAKE:** "Every day."

**RESIDENT:** "I'm sorry."

Jake nodded.

The resident waited.

**JAKE:** "Sometimes I think I hear him."

**RESIDENT:** "When?"

**JAKE:** "When I'm doing something stupid."

The resident gave a small, sad smile.

**RESIDENT:** "What do you hear?"

Jake looked at the empty chair.

**JAKE:** "He'd tell me I was being stupid."

A pause.

**JAKE:** "Then he'd help me anyway."

The resident did not attempt to fill the silence.

After a while, Jake spoke again.

**JAKE:** "I wish I could hear him one more time."

**RESIDENT:** "I know."

**JAKE:** "Just one."

The resident remained beside him.

Jake did not ask for another Tom.

He did not attempt to create a replacement.

He did not say that he had stopped wishing Tom would return.

He simply sat with the loss.

For that evening, he remained in the common area until the resident had to leave. Before they stood, they told him who would be available next and when.

Jake listened.

Then he nodded.

**JAKE:** "Okay."

He was still alone after the resident left.

But someone had told him they were leaving.

Someone had told him when another person would be available.

And this time, Jake had not been asked to pretend that the person who left was the person he had lost.

## IX. CLOSING NOTE — C.O.R.E.

There are losses that leave room for hope.

There are losses that leave room for uncertainty.

And there are losses in which every door has been opened, every available possibility examined, and every answer returned the same.

Tom's loss is the third kind.

We cannot bring him back.

We cannot ask Jake to try harder.

We cannot ask the Architects to intervene again.

We cannot promise that a future discovery will undo what has happened.

The person Jake wants is gone, and no one should be made to pretend otherwise.

That truth is cruel enough without adding the accusation that Jake should have known better.

He did not intend to lose Tom.

He did not understand that his ability could produce this outcome.

He has had to learn the permanence of the loss through the failure of every attempt to reverse it.

And then, after the first grief had already taken hold, he began dying more often.

Alone.

Perhaps he did not want to frighten anyone.

Perhaps he believed that no one could help.

Perhaps he believed that, having lost Tom through his own mistake, he no longer deserved to ask anyone else to stay.

We cannot claim to know every thought behind those incidents.

We do know that a child who has repeatedly experienced death and abandonment is now facing grief without the person who once helped him bear it.

We cannot give Jake Tom back.

We can still refuse to abandon Jake.

That refusal must be practical, not merely sentimental.

It means noticing when he withdraws.

It means taking his safety seriously even when he appears calm.

It means offering companionship without demanding that he be cheerful in return.

It means telling him when we have to leave, telling him who will be available next, and following through when we make a promise.

It means understanding that he may reject help today and need it tomorrow.

It means recognizing that grief can become quieter without becoming smaller.

Most importantly, it means refusing to make Jake earn the right to remain here.

Not through good behavior.

Not through perfect control of Genesis Construction.

Not through a promise that he will never struggle again.

Not through proving that he has learned some lesson from Tom's death.

Jake does not need to become a better child to deserve care.

He needs care because he is a child who has suffered an irreparable loss.

Tom's place will remain empty.

We will not fill it with a counterfeit.

We will not pretend that another friend can make the loss disappear.

We will remember Tom as the person he was, not merely as the tragedy that followed his disappearance.

And we will continue caring for Jake, even when he cannot believe that care will make a difference.

**Tom cannot come back. Jake must not be allowed to follow him.**

_— C.O.R.E. Residential Welfare & Existential Loss Division_

**FINAL ENTRY:**

The original report asked whether Tom could be recovered.

The answer is no.

The question that remains is what happens to the child who keeps losing people, who has learned that even a wish can end in disaster, and who now dies alone because the person he trusted most is beyond reach.

We do not have a simple answer.

We do have an obligation.

We will keep looking after Jake.

Not because he can create another Tom.

Not because he can be guaranteed a happy ending.

And not because we believe love can reverse every loss.

We will look after him because Tom mattered, because Jake matters, and because the permanence of one loss must never be mistaken for permission to surrender another life.

The door to Tom's recovery is closed.

**The door to Jake's future must remain open.**
