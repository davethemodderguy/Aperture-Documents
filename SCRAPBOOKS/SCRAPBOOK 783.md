### **CORE-SAFEHOUSE / PERSONNEL CARE DIVISION**

# **ALICE LOCKWOOD — MATERNAL MEMORY LOSS**

**Classification:** LEVEL 3 – Internal Personnel  
**Subject:** Alice Lockwood  
**Age:** 6  
**Species:** Full-Body Rabbit Thiren  
**Status:** Active — Severe Emotional Withdrawal / Memory Loss

---

# **INCIDENT SUMMARY**

Alice has forgotten her mother.

The memory loss was initially identified when personnel reviewed Alice's personal journal.

The page previously dedicated to her mother remained present.

The drawing remained.

The handwriting remained.

The words remained.

But Alice no longer recognized the person depicted.

When shown the page, Alice stared at it for several seconds.

She then quietly asked:

> "...Who's that?"

Personnel explained that the drawing was of her mother.

Alice continued looking at the page.

There was no visible emotional reaction.

No tears.

No confusion.

No recognition.

Only silence.

---

# **EMOTIONAL RESPONSE**

Personnel had expected Alice to become distressed upon discovering that she could no longer remember her mother.

She did not.

Instead, Alice appeared almost completely emotionally detached.

She looked at the drawing.

Then at her hands.

Then back at the drawing.

Finally:

> "...Alice don't remember."

A pause.

> "...Okay."

She closed the journal.

And put it away.

---

# **CURRENT BEHAVIOR**

Since the memory loss, Alice has become noticeably more emotionally numb.

She rarely expresses excitement.

She rarely expresses fear.

She rarely expresses sadness outwardly.

She no longer asks about her mother.

She does not search for her.

She does not speak about her.

She does not appear to miss someone she can no longer remember.

This has produced an unsettling change in Alice's behavior.

Previously, Alice's sadness was obvious.

Now there is very little visible emotion at all.

Personnel have informally described the change as:

> **"Alice became heartless."**

C.O.R.E. medical staff reject the literal interpretation.

Alice has not lost the capacity for emotion.

Rather, she appears to have become **emotionally numb.**

---

# **BEHAVIORAL ASSESSMENT**

Alice continues to recognize people she currently knows.

She still responds to familiar voices.

She still accepts necessary medical care.

She still understands basic affection and safety.

However, her spontaneous emotional expression has diminished considerably.

She may sit in the same corner for long periods.

She may stare at her hands.

She may observe people without approaching them.

She rarely initiates conversation.

She rarely asks for anything.

She no longer seems particularly concerned with whether people notice her absence.

This last behavior is considered especially concerning.

---

# **THE JOURNAL**

The maternal journal page has not been removed.

C.O.R.E. has classified it as a protected personal item.

Personnel are forbidden from altering, hiding, or destroying it.

Alice's writing on the page remains unchanged.

The final lines still read:

> **"Mom loves Alice."**

> **"Alice loves Mom."**

> **"Alice don't remember Mom."**

> **"Alice is bad girl."**

The first two statements were written before the memory loss.

The final statements were written during Alice's earlier period of memory deterioration.

The page now represents something different.

It is no longer merely a reminder for Alice.

It is evidence of a relationship Alice once remembered.

---

# **CORIN'S RESPONSE**

Corin asked Alice whether she wanted to know what her mother was like.

Alice answered:

> "...No."

Corin asked why.

Alice looked toward the floor.

> "...If Alice don't remember..."

She paused.

> "...Maybe don't matter."

Corin immediately disagreed.

> "It matters."

Alice did not respond.

Corin continued:

> "Even if you can't remember her right now, it still matters."

Alice remained silent.

---

# **PERSONNEL CONCERN**

The greatest concern is not simply that Alice has lost a specific memory.

It is that Alice appears to have stopped fighting against the loss.

Previously, Alice desperately wanted to remember people.

She wrote reminders in her journal.

She wrote:

> **"DO NOT FORGET."**

> **"KEEP IT THAT WAY."**

She wanted to preserve every friendship she had.

Now she has begun responding to memory loss with indifference.

This may represent emotional exhaustion.

After repeated experiences of being forgotten, abandoned, injured, and frightened, Alice may have begun protecting herself by becoming less emotionally invested.

If she does not expect anything—

nothing can be taken away.

---

# **CURRENT DIRECTIVE**

Personnel must not attempt to force Alice to remember her mother.

Do not repeatedly show her photographs.

Do not demand recognition.

Do not say:

> "You should remember her."

Do not treat failure to recognize her mother as a behavioral failure.

Instead:

- Preserve Alice's memories and belongings safely.
    
- Allow Alice to ask questions naturally.
    
- Provide simple factual information if she asks.
    
- Continue providing consistent care.
    
- Maintain familiar routines.
    
- Encourage safe social interaction without forcing it.
    
- Monitor further memory loss.
    
- Monitor changes in emotional responsiveness.
    
- Continue medical and psychological evaluation.
    

Alice must not be made to feel guilty for forgetting.

---

# **FINAL OBSERVATION**

Alice used to have a page in her journal for her mother.

It was messy.

Childlike.

Full of love.

She wrote that her mother loved her.

She wrote that she loved her mother.

And then she wrote that she could not remember her.

Now Alice cannot even remember why the page was important.

She looks at the drawing of a woman who was once one of the most important people in her world and sees a stranger.

There is no anger.

No grief she can articulate.

Just:

> **"...Who's that?"**

And perhaps that is more frightening than crying.

Alice has spent so long believing that caring makes her vulnerable that she is beginning to stop caring altogether.

Not because she is cruel.

Not because she has truly become heartless.

But because a child who has been hurt enough times may eventually decide that feeling nothing is safer than losing something else.

C.O.R.E. will not accept that as the end of Alice's story.

She does not need to remember her mother immediately.

She does not need to smile.

She does not need to become the Alice she was before.

For now, she simply needs people to remain beside her.

Quietly.

Consistently.

Without asking anything in return.

Because even if Alice has forgotten someone she once loved—

**she has not forgotten how to be loved.**
