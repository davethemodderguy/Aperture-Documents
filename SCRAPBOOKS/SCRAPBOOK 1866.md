## I. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

Ten weeks have elapsed since Tom Akana's permanent demanifestation.

During that period, Jake Akana has died more than **5,000 times**.

The number remains subject to reconciliation because some incidents were unwitnessed, some were discovered only after a delay, and the precise beginning and end of certain episodes could not be established. The current figure represents the number of recorded death events attributed to Jake during the reporting period, not a verified count of every event that may have occurred.

The average recorded rate exceeds seventy deaths per day.

This is no longer adequately described as a series of individual incidents.

It is a sustained, catastrophic crisis.

Jake's ability to reconstruct his body after death has allowed him to return from many of these events. It has not prevented them. His resurrection remains inconsistent in its activation timing, and every incident carries medical and psychological risks that cannot be dismissed merely because he has survived previous ones.

The pattern also represents a profound failure of the existing residential response.

Despite repeated welfare interventions, increased observation, and the establishment of a distributed support plan, Jake's condition has continued to deteriorate. The loss of Tom remains central to the chronology, but C.O.R.E. cannot assume it is the only factor contributing to every incident.

The current record supports three conclusions:

1. Jake is experiencing an ongoing, life-threatening crisis that has not been brought under control.
    
2. His anomalous capacity to return from death is not functioning as a reliable safeguard against further harm.
    
3. The current care arrangement has failed to provide sufficient protection, regardless of the good intentions of the individuals involved.
    

The matter has been escalated to executive emergency status.

**The priority is no longer to document how often Jake dies. It is to stop treating another death as an event that can be managed after it happens.**

That reaction is understandable.

It is also dangerous.

Each recorded event represents another occasion on which Jake's life ended, another uncertain interval before possible reconstruction, and another opportunity for a complication that his anomalous abilities may not overcome.

A death count is useful for identifying the scale of the crisis. It is not an adequate description of the child experiencing it.

C.O.R.E. therefore prohibits the use of the figure as a joke, an informal benchmark, or evidence that Jake has become accustomed to death.

No one may describe the events as harmless because Jake has survived them.

No one may tell Jake that the number makes his suffering ordinary.

And no one may treat the next incident as inevitable.

**Five thousand deaths are not five thousand successful recoveries. They are five thousand failures to prevent the crisis from reaching that point.**

## III. WEEK-BY-WEEK REVIEW

The available records do not support a precise weekly count in every category. The following summary documents the established progression without fabricating an exact distribution.

### Weeks One and Two — Acute Bereavement

The first period following Tom's loss was characterized by withdrawal, grief, repeated attempts to understand the irreversible nature of the demanifestation, and increasing difficulty initiating contact with others.

Jake stopped expecting Tom to return after C.O.R.E. confirmed that no recovery method existed.

He did not stop missing him.

During this period, Jake began dying more frequently. The initial incidents were treated as separate emergencies, although the pattern was already concerning.

### Weeks Three and Four — Escalation

The frequency of incidents increased.

Jake increasingly withdrew from ordinary companionship. Invitations were sometimes accepted, sometimes refused, and sometimes met with silence.

Staff members reported difficulty determining when Jake was simply seeking privacy and when he was in acute distress.

The existing support schedule did not reliably interrupt the escalation.

### Weeks Five and Six — Loss of Predictability

The intervals between incidents became increasingly difficult to interpret.

The medical team could not establish a dependable pattern that would allow personnel to predict when another event might occur.

The resurrection mechanism continued to produce uncertainty. Some revivals occurred after delays; others were discovered too late for the onset to be reliably documented.

Personnel became increasingly concerned that the apparent normality between incidents was concealing continued distress.

### Weeks Seven and Eight — Crisis Normalization

By this stage, the sheer number of incidents had begun to distort the residential response.

Staff members were exhausted. Residents were frightened. Some had become hesitant to leave Jake alone, while others were unsure how to approach him without provoking further distress.

Several residents expressed fear that nothing they did could help.

The result was an increasingly unstable situation in which Jake's need for dependable care existed alongside the exhaustion of the people attempting to provide it.

Neither problem could be solved by asking everyone to try harder indefinitely.

### Weeks Nine and Ten — Emergency Escalation

The recorded count surpassed 5,000.

At this point, the previous intervention model could no longer be considered adequate.

The issue was not a lack of documentation. It was not a lack of concern. It was not the absence of people who loved Jake.

The issue was that the available support had not stopped the repeated deaths.

C.O.R.E. has therefore suspended any assumption that the current plan can continue with minor adjustments.

A comprehensive review is required.

## IV. INCIDENT REVIEW — THE ABSENT PERSON

Tom's demanifestation remains the defining loss in the chronology.

The available evidence does not establish that every subsequent death is directly caused by grief. Jake's condition is complex, and the precise circumstances preceding individual events are not always known.

However, the loss has changed the context in which Jake experiences companionship.

Tom was not simply a friend who happened to be available.

Tom was the person who had witnessed Jake's earlier deaths and feared losing him despite knowing that resurrection was possible. He had stayed beside Jake when the white fire failed to appear for more than four days. He had panicked, shouted, and cried when Jake finally returned.

Their relationship had become part of Jake's expectation of what it meant to have someone.

When Tom disappeared permanently, Jake lost not only a person he loved, but also the person whose presence had repeatedly contradicted his fear that everyone would eventually leave.

The subsequent incidents suggest that the loss has not been integrated into a stable understanding of his life.

Instead, Jake appears to be living through an ongoing crisis in which the future has become difficult to imagine without Tom.

C.O.R.E. must not assume that replacing Tom's companionship with a greater number of residents will resolve this.

It will not.

No one else is Tom.

No one can make that fact untrue.

The purpose of a support network is not to fill Tom's exact place. It is to keep Jake connected to the living, to provide dependable assistance, and to help him survive grief that may remain painful for a long time.

The distinction must be maintained even as the emergency response intensifies.

## V. RESIDENTIAL INTERVIEW — "YOU KEEP ASKING ME TO COME BACK"

**Interviewee:** Jake Akana  
**Interviewer:** C.O.R.E. Residential Welfare Specialist  
**Timing:** Following a medically stabilized incident  
**Transcript:** Edited for clarity; no inference should be treated as a verbatim statement beyond the recorded dialogue.

Jake was sitting quietly when the specialist entered.

A welfare officer remained nearby, outside his immediate personal space.

**SPECIALIST:** "Jake, can we talk for a little while?"

**JAKE:** "Do I have to?"

**SPECIALIST:** "No. You can ask for a break at any point. I do need to check how you're doing."

**JAKE:** "I'm fine."

The specialist waited.

**SPECIALIST:** "You have had a very difficult ten weeks."

**JAKE:** "I know."

**SPECIALIST:** "Can you tell me what has been the hardest part?"

Jake looked down at his hands.

**JAKE:** "Everyone keeps asking me to talk."

**SPECIALIST:** "I'm sorry. Does it feel like too much?"

**JAKE:** "They keep asking if I miss Tom."

**SPECIALIST:** "And you do."

**JAKE:** "Of course I do."

His voice sharpened.

**JAKE:** "He's gone. What else am I supposed to say?"

The specialist nodded.

**SPECIALIST:** "You don't have to find a new answer every time someone asks."

Jake was quiet for several seconds.

**JAKE:** "They keep saying they'll be there for me."

**SPECIALIST:** "They want to help."

**JAKE:** "Then they go away."

The specialist did not immediately respond.

**JAKE:** "They have things to do. They get tired. They have their own rooms and their own lives."

He swallowed.

**JAKE:** "I'm not angry at them for that."

**SPECIALIST:** "You can be angry, even if you understand why they leave."

Jake shook his head.

**JAKE:** "Tom had things to do, too."

The specialist remained silent.

**JAKE:** "But he was Tom."

His eyes filled with tears.

**JAKE:** "Now everyone keeps telling me that I'm not alone, but they're not him."

**SPECIALIST:** "You're right. They aren't."

Jake looked up, surprised by the agreement.

**SPECIALIST:** "We shouldn't pretend they can replace him."

**JAKE:** "Then why do they keep trying?"

**SPECIALIST:** "I think they're frightened for you. They want to help, and they don't always know what to say."

Jake rubbed his face.

**JAKE:** "I'm tired."

**SPECIALIST:** "I believe you."

**JAKE:** "I'm tired of waking up."

The specialist took a breath.

**SPECIALIST:** "When you say that, are you thinking about dying again?"

Jake did not answer immediately.

The specialist did not rush him.

**JAKE:** "...Sometimes."

**SPECIALIST:** "Thank you for telling me. I'm taking that seriously. We need to make sure you're safe right now."

Jake looked away.

**JAKE:** "I don't want another lecture."

**SPECIALIST:** "You won't get one from me."

**JAKE:** "I don't want everyone watching me like I'm going to break."

**SPECIALIST:** "I understand. We need to keep you safe without making you feel like you've lost every bit of privacy."

**JAKE:** "Can you do that?"

The specialist did not promise perfection.

**SPECIALIST:** "We need to work out how. I won't pretend we've got it right yet."

Jake sat in silence.

Then, very quietly:

**JAKE:** "I just want one day where I don't have to be Jake Akana."

**SPECIALIST:** "What would you like to be instead?"

Jake thought about it.

**JAKE:** "A kid."

The interview was paused.

The statement was entered into the record as a significant expression of distress. Jake's wish was not interpreted as a request to abandon his identity, but as an indication that his life had become dominated by the burden of surviving his own crisis.

C.O.R.E. must respond accordingly.

## VI. EMERGENCY DIRECTIVES

### Directive 01 — Suspend Routine Handling of Repeated Deaths

All subsequent incidents involving Jake are to be treated as individual medical emergencies.

No death is to be dismissed as another instance of a familiar pattern.

No response team may wait for the resurrection mechanism to activate before initiating appropriate emergency care.

### Directive 02 — Immediate Comprehensive Clinical Review

A multidisciplinary team will reassess Jake's physical condition, anomalous physiology, psychological state, and environmental safety.

The review must consider the cumulative impact of repeated death and reconstruction, including respiratory complications and the uncertainty of revival timing.

No procedure may deliberately induce death to test the mechanism.

### Directive 03 — Strengthen Crisis Support

Jake must have clear, low-effort ways to request immediate help, including when he cannot explain what he is feeling.

The support plan must include several designated contacts and a defined response pathway when the preferred contact is unavailable.

Personnel must not depend on Jake to initiate every intervention. Given the established pattern of incidents occurring alone, active and proportionate welfare checks are required.

### Directive 04 — Protect Privacy and Dignity

Safety measures must not become punishment.

Jake must not be subjected to public discussion of his death count, treated as a spectacle, or made to feel that every resident interaction is an assessment.

The number of recorded deaths is an operational indicator. It is not his identity.

### Directive 05 — Support the Residential Community

SAFEHOUSE residents and staff are showing signs of exhaustion and helplessness.

C.O.R.E. will distribute responsibilities, provide appropriate support to those affected, and ensure that Jake's safety does not depend on one person's continuous presence.

Residents must be encouraged to maintain reasonable boundaries while using clear handoffs to ensure that Jake is not left without access to help during a crisis.

### Directive 06 — No Replacement for Tom

No resident is to be asked to imitate Tom, reproduce his behavior, or assume his emotional role in Jake's life.

Jake must be allowed to grieve Tom as a unique individual.

Care must build new connections without pretending that those connections erase the loss.

### Directive 07 — Executive Accountability

The current intervention model has not stopped the crisis.

C.O.R.E. will review why the existing measures have been insufficient, including whether the response has focused too heavily on managing individual incidents rather than preventing recurrence.

The purpose of this review is not to assign blame to exhausted residents.

It is to change a system that is not adequately protecting a child in sustained danger.

## VII. C.O.R.E. ASSESSMENT — FIVE THOUSAND IS NOT A MILESTONE

There is a temptation to look at the number and ask how Jake could possibly have endured it.

There is another temptation to look at the number and assume that, because he has endured it, he can continue.

Both interpretations are dangerous.

Survival is not evidence that suffering has become tolerable.

Repeated reconstruction is not proof that the body has escaped consequences.

A child who returns from death five thousand times is not necessarily a child who has become comfortable with death.

He may be a child who has lost the ability to imagine another way out.

We cannot know from the count alone what Jake experiences during every incident. We cannot assume that each event has the same cause, meaning, or subjective experience.

But we can no longer treat the pattern as an unexpected series of emergencies.

Ten weeks have passed.

More than five thousand deaths have been recorded.

The interventions so far have not been enough.

That is the fact C.O.R.E. must confront.

The response must become more effective, more coordinated, and more attentive to the life Jake is trying to endure between incidents.

It is not sufficient to tell him that people love him.

It is not sufficient to ask him to talk about his grief.

It is not sufficient to ensure that someone is nearby after he has already died.

He needs support that reaches him before the crisis, during the moments when he begins to feel unsafe, and after an incident when the physical and emotional consequences remain.

He needs a way to ask for help without feeling that he is inconveniencing someone.

He needs care that does not depend on whether he is cheerful, cooperative, or able to explain himself.

And he needs a future that is not measured exclusively by the number of times he survives another day.

## VIII. CLOSING NOTE — C.O.R.E.

Ten weeks ago, Tom Akana disappeared.

We confirmed that no one could bring him back.

We told Jake the truth, because false hope would have been another form of harm.

We thought that, once the search ended, the next task would be helping him grieve.

We underestimated how much of Jake's world had been built around the person he lost.

Five thousand deaths later, we can no longer describe this as grief progressing at its own pace.

We cannot say that Jake is healing simply because he sometimes smiles.

We cannot say that he is safe because the white fire has appeared again.

We cannot say that the people around him have done enough simply because they have tried.

Good intentions matter.

They do not make an ineffective response effective.

Tom cannot return. That fact is permanent.

But Jake's future is not yet determined, and we must stop behaving as though each new death is merely another consequence of a loss we cannot undo.

We do not need to undo Tom's disappearance to help Jake.

We do not need to replace Tom to remain beside Jake.

We do not need to promise Jake that he will never be lonely again.

We need to help him remain alive long enough to discover that his life can still contain things other than loss.

That will not happen through a single conversation, a single schedule, or a single declaration of love.

It will require sustained care, honest boundaries, active intervention, and a willingness to change course when what we are doing fails.

Jake once asked for a day when he would not have to be Jake Akana.

A day when he could simply be a kid.

That request should not be difficult to understand.

He is exhausted from surviving. He is exhausted from being watched. He is exhausted from losing the person who understood him best and from being asked to explain a grief that no explanation can repair.

He should be allowed to play.

He should be allowed to laugh.

He should be allowed to refuse a conversation.

He should be allowed to miss Tom without having to turn that loss into a lesson.

And he should be allowed to ask for help before he reaches the point where he no longer believes help is possible.

**Five thousand deaths are not proof that Jake cannot be saved. They are proof that our current response has not saved him from this pattern.**

The distinction is not comforting.

It is an obligation.
