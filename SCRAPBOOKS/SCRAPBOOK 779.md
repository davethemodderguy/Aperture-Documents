# **APERTURE INDUSTRIES**

## **C.O.R.E. – CENTRAL OPERATIONS & RESEARCH EXECUTIVE**

### **CORE-SAFEHOUSE / PERSONNEL CARE DIVISION**

# **ALICE LOCKWOOD — RAIN INCIDENT / PSYCHOLOGICAL SETBACK**

**Classification:** LEVEL 10 — CRITICAL CHILD SAFETY  
**Incident Type:** Repeated Unintentional Isolation  
**Status:** Recovered / Psychological Monitoring Required

---

# **INCIDENT SUMMARY**

Alice was forgotten outside again.

It was raining heavily.

She was not supposed to be outside alone.

Someone had been with her.

They returned inside.

Alice did not.

The door closed.

And nobody noticed.

---

# **THE RAIN**

Alice remained outside for an extended period.

Her white beanie became completely soaked.

Her hoodie and coat were saturated.

Her boots filled with water.

Her hair clung to her face.

Her rabbit ears hung limply beneath the weight of the water.

She was trembling violently from the cold.

Her small body was exhausted.

Yet she remained where she was.

---

# **SHE CAN SPEAK NOW**

This was the part that initially confused investigators.

Alice has her voice back.

She can speak.

She can ask for help.

She knows how to say:

> "Help."

She knows how to say:

> "Someone come here."

She knows how to call for Corin.

She simply didn't.

---

# **WHY?**

No definitive explanation has been established.

Alice was asked why she didn't call out.

She remained silent for several seconds.

Then she quietly answered:

> "...Didn't want to bother."

The investigator asked:

> "You were cold."

Alice nodded.

> "You were alone."

Alice nodded.

> "Why didn't you call someone?"

Alice looked down.

> "...They busy."

---

# **THE POSSIBILITY OF DELIBERATE INTERFERENCE**

Personnel initially considered the possibility that Alice had been deliberately prevented from calling for help.

No evidence of physical restraint was found.

No injuries consistent with restraint were discovered.

No confirmed evidence of deliberate silencing has been identified.

The possibility has therefore **not been ruled out**, but remains unsubstantiated.

Security has opened an investigation.

---

# **THE MORE DISTURBING POSSIBILITY**

Medical personnel believe there is another explanation.

Alice may have learned that being forgotten is something she is expected to endure quietly.

Her history contains too many similar incidents.

She has been:

- Forgotten outside.
    
- Left in rain.
    
- Left in snow.
    
- Locked inside rooms.
    
- Forgotten in elevators.
    
- Left inside storage areas.
    
- Forgotten in the cafeteria.
    
- Nearly exposed to the Anti-Rodent System.
    
- Forgotten during walks.
    
- Left behind for hours.
    
- Hidden when frightened.
    
- Found after prolonged isolation.
    

Each incident taught her something.

Not necessarily consciously.

But the pattern remained.

---

# **THE LEARNED RESPONSE**

Alice's previous response to being forgotten was:

> **Wait.**

If nobody came—

wait longer.

If she became cold—

wait.

If she became tired—

sit down.

If she was scared—

hide.

If she believed she was bothering someone—

be quieter.

Her newly recovered voice did not automatically erase that behavior.

---

# **CORIN'S DISCOVERY**

Corin eventually found her.

Alice was sitting beneath a tree.

She was soaked through.

Her body was trembling.

Her ears were completely limp.

Her scooter was lying nearby.

Her face was expressionless.

Corin immediately ran toward her.

> "Alice!"

Alice looked up.

Her voice was barely audible.

> "...Big sis."

Corin picked her up.

Alice immediately curled against her.

---

# **NO COMPLAINT**

Alice didn't say:

> "Why did you leave me?"

She didn't say:

> "I'm angry."

She didn't say:

> "I was scared."

She didn't blame anyone.

She simply whispered:

> "...Sorry."

Corin froze.

> "Why are you apologizing?"

Alice didn't answer.

---

# **PSYCHOLOGICAL ASSESSMENT**

The incident represents a significant psychological setback.

Alice's existing fear of being forgotten has been reinforced.

She has begun displaying several concerning behaviors:

- Increased withdrawal.
    
- Reduced spontaneous conversation.
    
- Increased hesitation before approaching people.
    
- Repeatedly asking whether she is "bothering" others.
    
- Increased reluctance to follow people.
    
- Increased tendency to wait silently when separated.
    
- Heightened fear of closed doors.
    
- Increased attachment to familiar people.
    
- Difficulty believing that someone will return when they leave.
    

Her previous progress in social confidence has noticeably regressed.

---

# **THE QUESTION**

One member of the medical team asked Alice:

> "If you get left outside again, what should you do?"

Alice answered:

> "Wait."

The doctor shook their head.

> "No."

Alice looked confused.

> "You call someone."

Alice remained silent.

> "Alice?"

After several seconds:

> "...What if they're busy?"

The doctor answered:

> "They aren't too busy to help you."

Alice didn't appear convinced.

---

# **CORIN'S RESPONSE**

Corin sat beside Alice afterward.

> "If you need me, you call me."

Alice looked down.

> "Even if you're busy?"

> "Especially then."

Alice hesitated.

> "Even if Alice bothers you?"

Corin immediately answered:

> "You don't bother me."

Alice's ears remained lowered.

Corin gently touched the top of her beanie.

> "You never have to earn the right to ask for help."

Alice finally whispered:

> "...Okay."

---

# **NEW SAFETY MEASURES**

Following the incident, the existing Alice supervision policy has been strengthened.

Alice must now have:

**A named responsible person.**

**Confirmed location checks.**

**Physical confirmation during transitions.**

**Immediate investigation if she cannot be located.**

Additionally:

### **NO ASSUMPTIONS**

Personnel may not assume Alice is:

> "with someone else."

> "probably inside."

> "probably at SAFEHOUSE."

> "probably following somebody."

If nobody can positively confirm where Alice is—

**she is considered missing.**

---

# **VOICE-USE TRAINING**

Alice will also be taught practical emergency phrases.

Not as punishment.

Not because she did anything wrong.

But because she deserves to know that she can make herself heard.

Training will include:

> **"Help!"**

> **"I'm outside!"**

> **"Please come back!"**

> **"I'm scared."**

> **"I'm cold."**

> **"I need someone."**

The goal is not to make Alice responsible for her own safety.

The goal is to give her another tool while the adults around her improve their own responsibility.

---

# **CORIN'S NOTE**

> "She has her voice back."

> "But she still thinks she isn't allowed to use it."

> "That's what scares me."

> "We've spent so much time teaching Alice that she has people who care about her."

> "Then something like this happens, and she goes right back to thinking she should just wait quietly."

> "We're not letting that become normal."

---

# **FINAL OBSERVATION**

Alice was not silent because she lacked a voice.

She was silent because somewhere along the way, she learned that nobody might come.

That is the part that must change.

Alice is five.

She should not have to calculate whether she is "bothering" someone before asking to be rescued from the rain.

She should not have to decide whether she is important enough to call for help.

She should not have to sit quietly and wait for someone to remember her.

And she certainly should not have to become invisible before anyone notices that she is missing.

The rain incident did not physically break Alice.

But psychologically—

it hurt.

And everyone around her understands now that repairing that damage will take more than simply telling her:

> **"We'll remember you next time."**

They have to prove it.

Every day.
