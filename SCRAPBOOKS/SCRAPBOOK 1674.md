# EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

L.S.I.N. has identified the anomalous mechanism underlying Clover's unusually complicated history of "luck."

The phenomenon is not generalized probability manipulation.

It does not make everyone around Clover lucky.

It does not protect nearby individuals.

It does not redistribute favorable outcomes among groups.

The effect is considerably more specific.

> **Clover is the beneficiary.**

Clover emits a persistent, passive anomalous field.

The field appears to influence probability and circumstance in ways that disproportionately favor **Clover herself.**

Individuals near her do not receive the same protection.

Objects do not become universally fortunate.

Events do not become beneficial for everyone.

If an accident occurs, Clover's anomalous field appears to preferentially select an outcome in which **Clover survives.**

Other individuals are not guaranteed the same outcome.

This provides a disturbing explanation for the most traumatic event of Clover's childhood.

Her father's death was not caused by Clover's field harming him.

Rather:

> **Clover's luck saved Clover.**

It did not save her father.

---

# 1. THE FIELD

L.S.I.N. initially suspected that Clover possessed an unusually broad probability-altering anomaly.

Testing has since produced a more specific model.

The field is:

- passive;
    
- continuously active;
    
- centered on Clover;
    
- apparently involuntary;
    
- primarily beneficial to Clover;
    
- not consciously directed;
    
- and not reliably transferable to others.
    

Clover does not appear to "activate" her luck.

She does not choose when it operates.

She does not appear to know exactly how it operates.

In practical terms, the phenomenon resembles a persistent anomalous preference in reality:

> **When circumstances permit multiple outcomes, reality appears disproportionately likely to select the outcome most favorable to Clover.**

This does not mean Clover is invulnerable.

She can still:

- trip;
    
- fall;
    
- become injured;
    
- lose objects;
    
- make mistakes;
    
- experience illness;
    
- suffer emotional trauma;
    
- and experience severe personal loss.
    

The field does not make her life good.

It appears to make her **survival disproportionately likely.**

There is a difference.

---

# 2. THE FATHER

The childhood accident has been reassessed.

At approximately six years old, Clover was involved in an accident that resulted in her father's death.

Clover survived.

Her mother interpreted the event as evidence that Clover brought bad luck to others.

The original interpretation was incorrect.

Clover did not cause her father's death through anomalous influence.

The available evidence instead suggests:

> **The same event that killed her father produced a favorable survival outcome for Clover.**

Her anomaly did not extend its protection to him.

It selected her.

This explains a question that has remained unresolved for nearly two decades:

### Why did Clover survive?

Because her luck was protecting **her.**

### Why did her father die?

Because the field was never protecting **him.**

---

# 3. THE CRUELTY OF THE EFFECT

This discovery has created an unusual ethical problem.

Clover's anomaly is beneficial.

But its benefit is inherently selfish.

Not intentionally.

Not maliciously.

Simply structurally.

If Clover and another person are placed in the same dangerous situation, there is no evidence that her anomaly considers their survival equally important.

It may even favor Clover's survival at their expense.

Clover does not control this.

She did not choose it.

She did not know it existed when she was six.

She certainly did not understand it when her father died.

Nevertheless, she was blamed for the result.

---

# 4. HER MOTHER'S ACCUSATION

Clover's mother believed:

> **"You cause bad luck."**

Clover internalized this.

For years, she attempted to understand what she had done wrong.

She never found an answer.

There was no answer to find.

She was not responsible for the anomaly.

She did not kill her father.

She did not curse her family.

She did not intentionally cause an accident.

She survived it.

Her survival became the evidence used against her.

---

# 5. IDENTITY LOSS

The psychological consequences are significantly more severe than previously documented.

Clover no longer remembers:

- her original name;
    
- her parents' faces;
    
- her childhood home;
    
- or several other fundamental details of her early life.
    

The name **Clover** is therefore not simply a nickname.

It has effectively become her primary identity.

Her original name has been lost.

Whether the memory loss is neurological, psychological, anomalous, or some combination remains under investigation.

C.O.R.E. Medical Research has not authorized invasive memory-recovery procedures.

---

# 6. THE NAME "CLOVER"

The irony has been noted.

Clover does not remember what her parents called her.

She remembers fragments.

Feelings.

Rooms.

Sounds.

A vague sense of warmth.

A man's presence.

A woman's voice.

But no reliable names.

No faces.

No complete family portrait.

She remembers being a child.

She does not remember **who that child was.**

The four-leaf clover pupils became the easiest identifier.

Eventually:

> **Clover became Clover.**

The name stayed.

Almost everything else did not.

---

# 7. NIGHTTIME

Clover has a severe fear response associated with nighttime.

This is not simply a dislike of darkness.

Her father's death occurred at night.

Her memory of the event is incomplete.

However, the emotional association remains.

Nighttime is therefore linked to:

- danger;
    
- helplessness;
    
- loss;
    
- her father's death;
    
- and the possibility that something terrible will happen again.
    

Clover becomes significantly more anxious after sunset.

She frequently checks doors.

She may remain awake until sunrise.

She often keeps a light nearby.

She does not like being unable to see her surroundings.

---

# 8. CHRONIC INSOMNIA

Clover has **chronic insomnia.**

The condition is aggravated by her fear of nighttime.

Sleep does not appear to be restorative enough to compensate for the accumulated exhaustion.

She may remain awake for extended periods.

She occasionally falls asleep unintentionally while drawing.

This is one of the few situations in which she appears able to relax sufficiently for sleep to occur.

C.O.R.E. has therefore recommended that drawing not be treated as a frivolous hobby.

It may function as one of Clover's coping mechanisms.

---

# 9. NIGHTSHADE RETROGRADE VISUAL IMPAIRMENT

Clover also possesses **Nightshade Retrograde Visual Impairment.**

Her visual acuity deteriorates substantially under low-light conditions.

The effect is most pronounced at night.

Clover can navigate normally during adequate illumination.

Once lighting becomes insufficient, however, objects become increasingly difficult to distinguish.

Depth perception becomes unreliable.

Movement becomes harder to track.

Familiar environments may become disorienting.

This is particularly problematic given her fear of nighttime.

Clover is therefore frequently observed carrying a candle.

---

# 10. THE CANDLE

The candle is not considered anomalous.

It is simply practical.

Clover often carries one when walking through SAFEHOUSE at night.

The small flame gives her enough illumination to distinguish:

- walls;
    
- doors;
    
- furniture;
    
- stairs;
    
- people;
    
- and nearby objects.
    

She prefers a candle to simply turning on every available light.

When asked why:

> **CLOVER:**  
> "It feels less empty."

No further clarification was provided.

Personnel have not pressed the matter.

---

# 11. PHOENIX'S FLOOR

Following resident welfare review, Clover has been assigned accommodation on **Phoenix's residential floor.**

The decision was influenced by several factors.

Most importantly:

> **Clover likes Phoenix.**

Clover was already familiar with Phoenix's medical reputation through KCA.

She considers Phoenix one of the most impressive medical personnel she has encountered.

The admiration appears genuine.

Clover has reportedly watched Phoenix work from a distance and occasionally drawn her.

Phoenix has been informed.

Her response was:

> **PHOENIX:**  
> "...She draws me?"

Pause.

> **KCA STAFF:**  
> "Apparently."

Another pause.

> **PHOENIX:**  
> "Why?"

The answer was apparently:

> **"Because you're cool."**

Phoenix reportedly did not know how to respond.

---

# 12. PHOENIX & CLOVER

Their relationship has been quiet.

Clover does not constantly seek Phoenix's attention.

She simply seems more comfortable knowing that Phoenix is nearby.

Phoenix, in turn, has not attempted to force Clover into conversation.

This arrangement appears beneficial to both residents.

Clover can draw.

Phoenix can work.

They can occupy the same general space without requiring constant interaction.

Occasionally:

> **CLOVER:**  
> "Can I draw you?"

Phoenix looks up.

> **PHOENIX:**  
> "Why?"

> **CLOVER:**  
> "I like your face."

Phoenix pauses.

> **PHOENIX:**  
> "...Fine."

Clover returns to her notebook.

---

# 13. DRAWING

Clover draws whenever she has time.

Her subjects vary.

Common examples include:

- plants;
    
- animals;
    
- residents;
    
- buildings;
    
- landscapes;
    
- accounting diagrams;
    
- abstract shapes;
    
- four-leaf clovers;
    
- Phoenix;
    
- and occasionally things she cannot consciously identify.
    

The unidentified drawings are of particular interest.

Several contain vague human silhouettes.

Clover cannot identify them.

When asked who they are:

> **CLOVER:**  
> "I don't know."

She usually continues drawing.

C.O.R.E. has instructed personnel not to force interpretation onto these images.

---

# 14. THE MOST CONCERNING DRAWINGS

Several drawings contain the same recurring elements.

A small child.

A taller male figure.

A dark background.

A bright green shape.

The child's face is always blank.

The man's face is always incomplete.

In one drawing, the child is holding the man's hand.

In another, the man's hand is absent.

In the most recent version, the child is standing alone.

Clover has never stated that the man is her father.

She does not remember his face.

She may not consciously recognize him.

The drawings may represent memory fragments.

They may also represent nothing more than artistic repetition.

C.O.R.E. will not decide for her.

---

# 15. CURRENT MEMORY STATE

Clover's memory of her childhood should be considered **fragmentary.**

She remembers:

> **She had parents.**

She remembers:

> **She loved them.**

She remembers:

> **Her father died.**

She remembers:

> **Her mother blamed her.**

She remembers:

> **Someone rescued her.**

She remembers:

> **That person eventually died.**

But she does not remember:

> **Her own original name.**

She does not remember:

> **Her parents' faces.**

She does not remember enough to reconstruct her childhood.

This may be the most severe form of loss currently documented among SAFEHOUSE residents.

Not simply losing people.

Not simply losing a home.

But losing the memories that prove those people ever existed.

---

# 16. RESIDENT WELFARE GUIDANCE

Personnel are instructed:

Do not ask Clover:

> "What was your real name?"

Do not repeatedly ask:

> "Do you remember your mother?"

Do not show her photographs without warning.

Do not tell her:

> "You should remember this."

Do not describe her father as the person she "killed."

Do not tell her that her luck caused his death.

Do not attempt to force memory recovery.

Do not leave her alone in complete darkness unless medically necessary.

If Clover becomes distressed after sunset, provide illumination first.

Then allow her to decide whether she wants company.

---

# 17. L.S.I.N. ANOMALOUS ASSESSMENT

The luck field presents an unusual containment consideration.

Clover is not dangerous by intent.

The anomaly is passive.

There is no evidence that she can weaponize it deliberately.

However, the field's preferential effect means that uncontrolled experiments involving Clover and other individuals could produce ethically unacceptable outcomes.

Accordingly:

> **Clover is not to be used as a probability-testing instrument.**

No personnel are permitted to deliberately place Clover into dangerous circumstances to determine whether her luck will protect her.

The fact that the anomaly saved her once does not justify asking it to do so again.

---

# 18. C.O.R.E. MEDICAL ASSESSMENT

Clover's primary current welfare concerns are:

### Physical

- Nightshade Retrograde Visual Impairment;
    
- chronic sleep deprivation;
    
- occasional clumsiness;
    
- nighttime disorientation.
    

### Psychological

- severe childhood grief;
    
- persistent survivor's guilt;
    
- fear of nighttime;
    
- long-term internalization of maternal blame;
    
- fragmented autobiographical memory;
    
- loss of personal identity;
    
- fear that her presence harms others.
    

The final concern requires particular attention.

Clover has spent approximately twenty years believing that surviving her father's death made her responsible for it.

Current anomalous evidence indicates the opposite.

She survived because her anomaly favored **her survival.**

She did not choose that outcome.

---

# 19. THE MOST IMPORTANT FINDING

Clover was not unlucky.

She was not cursed.

She did not kill her father.

She did not bring death to the scientist who rescued her.

She did not cause every tragedy that followed.

She was a child with an anomaly nobody understood.

Her luck protected her.

And everyone around her interpreted her survival as evidence that she was responsible.

That misunderstanding followed her for twenty years.

---

# C.O.R.E. CLOSING OBSERVATION

Clover's room is usually dark.

Not completely.

There is almost always a candle.

A small flame beside her drawing materials.

Green hair illuminated by orange light.

Four-leaf pupils reflecting the flame.

A notebook open in front of her.

Sometimes she draws people she remembers.

Sometimes she draws people she does not.

Sometimes she draws flowers.

Sometimes she draws nothing recognizable at all.

She has no photograph of her parents that she can look at and say:

> **"That's my mother."**

She has no face she can look at and say:

> **"That's my father."**

She does not even know what name her parents gave her.

The child they called by that name is effectively gone.

All she has left is Clover.

A name other people gave her.

A name based on her eyes.

A name attached to an anomaly that kept her alive.

And perhaps that is why she keeps drawing.

Because if she cannot remember the faces...

she can still make new ones.

Phoenix was asked whether she was comfortable having Clover on her residential floor.

She answered immediately.

> **PHOENIX:**  
> "Yes."

The interviewer asked why.

Phoenix thought for a moment.

> **PHOENIX:**  
> "She likes drawing."

Pause.

> **PHOENIX:**  
> "I like having someone around who doesn't require me to talk."

Another pause.

> **PHOENIX:**  
> "And if she wakes up at three in the morning..."

Phoenix looked toward the hallway.

> **PHOENIX:**  
> "...she shouldn't have to be alone."

No further statement was made.

Clover's luck saved her life.

It did not save her childhood.

It did not save her family.

It did not save the scientist who rescued her.

It did not save her memories.

It did not save her name.

But she is still here.

Twenty years after the accident.

Twenty years after being blamed.

Twenty years after losing the people she loved.

She is still here.

And this time, when something goes wrong around her...

**C.O.R.E. will not blame her for surviving it.**
