### 1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

Sora's relocation to SAFEHOUSE has improved his living conditions considerably. However, his adjustment to a normal residential environment remains difficult.

He is easily frightened.

Ordinary household activities, unfamiliar sounds, unexpected movements, and unfamiliar people can cause him to become visibly distressed. He often remains close to Jake, who has become his most consistent source of companionship.

Another significant development concerns his eating habits.

Sora is only now beginning to eat proper meals.

These are among his first experiences with regularly receiving prepared food rather than scavenging discarded cafeteria leftovers. Even something as ordinary as sitting down to eat appears to be unfamiliar territory.

Additionally, Sora struggles to remember things.

His memory difficulties affect his ability to retain information and recall details. The exact extent and cause remain undetermined.

His collar remains one of the few consistent identifiers associated with him.

It bears a single name:

**SORA.**

### 2. OBSERVATIONS — A CHILD EASILY FRIGHTENED

Sora's fear response remains pronounced.

He can become frightened by situations that other residents would consider routine. Personnel are advised not to assume that familiarity with SAFEHOUSE automatically means familiarity with its environment.

A raised voice, a sudden approach, or an unexpected noise may be enough to make him retreat.

His current behavior includes:

- Staying close to Jake whenever possible.
    
- Retreating into his wall-integrated den when overwhelmed.
    
- Using his whiteboard rather than attempting verbal communication.
    
- Becoming hesitant around unfamiliar residents.
    
- Reacting cautiously to unfamiliar food and household routines.
    

These behaviors should not be treated as misbehavior.

Sora has spent a considerable amount of time learning that being noticed by people can lead to frightening experiences.

SAFEHOUSE must teach him something different through consistency, not demands.

### 3. FOOD FAMILIARIZATION — LEARNING TO EAT PROPERLY

<badge color="warning">ADJUSTMENT IN PROGRESS</badge>

Sora's relationship with food requires particular attention.

Before his relocation, he relied on discarded cafeteria leftovers. He would sneak into the cafeteria at night and eat whatever had been thrown away.

He did not have the luxury of choosing meals.

He did not have the luxury of expecting food to be prepared for him.

He did not even have the luxury of eating without worrying that someone might frighten him away.

Now, for the first time in a long while, Sora is being offered proper meals.

This is not an insignificant transition.

C.O.R.E. has observed that Sora remains hesitant around ordinary food. Staff must not interpret unfamiliarity or hesitation as ingratitude.

He is learning a routine that most children take for granted:

- Food is prepared for him.
    
- He is allowed to eat at a table.
    
- He does not have to compete with anyone.
    
- Nobody is waiting to scare him away.
    
- He can stop eating and return later if necessary.
    
- There is no punishment for leaving food unfinished.
    

Food must never become a test of obedience.

Sora should be offered small, manageable portions and familiar options where possible. He must also retain access to adequate nutrition and medical monitoring.

**He should not have to finish a meal to prove that he deserves the next one.**

### 4. MEMORY OBSERVATIONS — DIFFICULTIES WITH RECALL

Sora struggles to remember things.

The impairment appears broad enough that personnel should not rely on him remembering instructions, names, events, or previously established routines without assistance.

The precise nature of this difficulty has not yet been established.

C.O.R.E. will not assign a diagnosis based solely on observation.

However, several accommodations are recommended:

1. Use short, clear instructions.
    
2. Repeat important information without expressing frustration.
    
3. Maintain consistent routines.
    
4. Use visual reminders where possible.
    
5. Allow Sora to write information on his whiteboard.
    
6. Avoid testing his memory in front of other residents.
    
7. Never shame him for forgetting something he was previously told.
    

His whiteboard should be treated as an essential communication and memory aid, not merely a substitute for speech.

A particularly important consideration is that Sora may forget information without forgetting how someone made him feel.

Personnel must therefore remain patient and consistent even when an interaction appears to be happening for the second or third time.

### 5. THE COLLAR — HIS NAME

Sora's collar has a name written on it.

**SORA.**

It is a simple detail, but an important one.

The collar provides a consistent identifier, particularly when Sora has difficulty remembering information about himself or his surroundings.

It must be preserved and maintained.

No personnel are authorized to remove or replace it without an appropriate welfare or medical reason and a careful explanation to Sora.

His name should be used consistently.

Do not give him an alternative nickname without his agreement. Do not turn his memory difficulties into an excuse to disregard his identity.

Even when he struggles to remember everything, the people around him can remember him.

### 6. FLOOR 7 — A PLACE TO LEARN

Sora's Den remains available inside the small wall-integrated room on Floor 7.

It is not a place he should be encouraged to abandon simply because he is now living somewhere safe.

He may retreat there whenever he needs quiet.

Jake continues to spend considerable time with him. Sora is frequently found beside Jake, sometimes being carried around the floor.

This companionship appears to provide Sora with reassurance.

However, SAFEHOUSE residents must help establish a broader environment of safety so that Sora can gradually become comfortable around others, too.

Jake should remain his friend, not his only source of security.

### 7. RESIDENT GUIDANCE

All Floor 7 residents and SAFEHOUSE personnel are instructed to observe the following:

- Approach Sora slowly and announce your presence.
    
- Avoid sudden loud noises near his den.
    
- Do not force him into social activities.
    
- Offer proper food without pressure.
    
- Keep his collar and whiteboard accessible.
    
- Repeat information patiently.
    
- Do not laugh when he becomes frightened.
    
- Do not treat his memory difficulties as childish stubbornness.
    
- Let him experience ordinary childhood activities at his own pace.
    

There is no deadline for becoming comfortable.

There is no performance requirement for being a resident.

---

### 8. C.O.R.E. CLOSING NOTE

Sora is learning things that most children never have to think about.

That food can be prepared for him.

That he can eat without watching the door.

That a room can be his without having to defend it.

That someone can approach him without intending to frighten him.

And that forgetting something does not mean he has failed.

He does not seem to have had much of a childhood. At least, not one in which he could simply be a child.

We cannot give him back the time he lost.

We cannot promise that he will remember everything.

We cannot undo what happened to his voice.

But we can give him a place where he can write whatever he wants to say. We can make sure his meals are waiting. We can keep his little den warm. We can remember his name when he struggles to remember things himself.

His collar says Sora.

We should make sure the world around him says it, too.
