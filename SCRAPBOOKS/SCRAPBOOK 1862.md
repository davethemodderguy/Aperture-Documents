## I. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

Following the discovery that Jake Akana's resurrection mechanism may require an external physical stimulus to activate, C.O.R.E. conducted a further review of the psychological circumstances surrounding his seven-day crisis.

Two statements, independently made by Tom Akana and Aries Akana, have substantially changed the interpretation of the incidents.

Tom's statement identifies a central problem that conventional residential intervention may fail to address: **Jake does not merely want company. He is terrified of being left alone, and no number of friends, family members, or Genesis Construction manifestations can guarantee that he will never experience that loneliness again.**

Aries's statement identifies a second, deeper contradiction: Jake's existence was reportedly brought about by a sincere wish that reached a Blue Dwarf, a star capable of granting wishes. He was made to spread unconditional love, yet struggles to extend that same love toward himself.

Neither statement suggests that Jake's circumstances are hopeless. Both suggest that the previous response to his distress was insufficient because it treated loneliness as a scheduling problem rather than a trauma-related fear.

The residents cannot promise to be available at every moment. Genesis Construction cannot reliably solve the problem by producing more people. Tom cannot personally remain beside Jake every hour of every day without sacrificing his own childhood and wellbeing.

And Jake cannot be expected to solve his suffering by creating someone who will never leave.

The objective must therefore change.

C.O.R.E. will not attempt to guarantee that Jake is never alone. It will work to ensure that being alone does not automatically become, in Jake's mind, proof that he has been abandoned, forgotten, or deemed unworthy of love.

This distinction is now considered essential to his long-term care.

## II. STATEMENT ONE — TOM AKANA

> "No matter how many people he summons with Genesis Construction, no matter how many friends or family he have, they will get very busy eventually. He can't bear being alone.
> 
> He was starved to death and left staring at his body for 75 years... You think that wasn't tramuatizing enough for him.
> 
> None of us can give him company. Nor he can make someone stay for him."

**Source:** Tom Akana  
**Context:** Post-incident residential review  
**Transcript status:** Verbatim statement as recorded

### 2.1. The Seventy-Five Years

The most significant detail in Tom's statement is not the number of people Jake can summon.

It is the seventy-five years.

Jake's history includes a period in which he was starved to death and left staring at his own body for seventy-five years. The experience was not simply a period of solitude. It involved prolonged exposure to the physical evidence of his own death, with no meaningful intervention to end his suffering.

For an ordinary person, isolation can be distressing.

For a child who has already experienced an extended period of helplessness, death, and abandonment, isolation may carry a different meaning entirely.

An empty room may not feel empty.

It may feel familiar.

An unanswered request to play may not register as a minor disappointment. It may become evidence that everyone has somewhere better to be, that nobody will return, or that asking for companionship was a mistake.

C.O.R.E. cannot establish that every instance of Jake's distress is directly caused by this earlier experience. However, the severity of his past makes it unreasonable to treat his present fear of solitude as ordinary childish impatience.

**His reaction must be understood in the context of what solitude has meant to him before.**

### 2.2. Genesis Construction Is Not a Cure for Abandonment

Genesis Construction permits Jake to manifest imagined creations into reality. Its practical limits remain incompletely understood.

The ability creates an apparent solution to loneliness: if Jake wants someone beside him, he can create someone.

Tom rejects that solution.

His reasoning is straightforward. Anyone Jake creates may eventually have other obligations, become unavailable, or leave the room. Even a large circle of friends and family cannot eliminate the possibility of a quiet afternoon, an unanswered invitation, or a night in which everyone is asleep.

Creating more people may increase the number of relationships available to Jake. It cannot guarantee the permanent availability of every person in his life.

Nor should C.O.R.E. encourage Jake to treat manifested people as objects whose purpose is to remain beside him indefinitely. Any person created through Genesis Construction must be treated according to their actual status and autonomy, not merely as a solution to Jake's fear.

The distinction is crucial.

Jake may be able to create company.

He cannot manufacture a guarantee that nobody will ever need to leave.

### 2.3. Tom's Conclusion — And Its Limitation

Tom concludes that none of them can give Jake company, and that Jake cannot make someone stay for him.

Taken literally, this sounds like an admission of defeat.

It is not.

Tom is identifying a limit that the adults around Jake have not adequately acknowledged: **love does not grant anyone unlimited availability.**

However, the statement also contains the exhaustion of a child who has repeatedly watched someone he loves die, return, and suffer.

Tom knows that he cannot promise to be present at every moment. He knows that he has commitments of his own. He also knows what happens when he returns and finds Jake unresponsive.

The seven-day incident has left him carrying two seemingly incompatible fears:

- If he leaves, Jake may be alone when he needs someone.
    
- If he never leaves, Tom's own life becomes organized entirely around preventing the next crisis.
    

Neither outcome is acceptable.

Tom is Jake's companion, not his sole emergency service. He is Jake's family, not a substitute for an entire residential support system.

C.O.R.E. must not convert Tom's devotion into an obligation to remain permanently vigilant.

He deserves to be a child beside his friend.

He deserves to play, become distracted, attend to his own responsibilities, and return home without feeling that every hour spent elsewhere might cost Jake his life.

**Tom cannot be made responsible for proving that Jake is worth staying alive for.**

That responsibility does not belong to one person. It does not belong to a child.

## III. STATEMENT TWO — ARIES AKANA

> "Someone wished for a kid, very sinserely that it moved a Blue Dwarf, a star that grants wishes, and they abandoned it. Made to spread unconditional love. Yet he hold none for his own."

**Source:** Aries Akana  
**Context:** Post-incident residential review  
**Transcript status:** Verbatim statement as recorded

### 3.1. A Wish That Became a Child

Aries's statement describes Jake's existence as the consequence of a sincere wish: a wish so powerful that it moved a Blue Dwarf, a star capable of granting wishes.

The available statement does not identify the wisher or establish the full circumstances of Jake's creation. No further assumptions are authorized.

What it does establish is the contradiction Aries sees in Jake's present condition.

Jake was made to spread unconditional love.

Yet Jake struggles to offer that love to himself.

The contradiction is especially painful because Jake's outward affection is not in question. He seeks companionship. He reaches for his friends. He wants to play, share experiences, and be included. His desire to be near others is persistent enough that repeated rejection, even unintentional rejection, can become devastating.

But the same child who readily seeks other people's company may struggle to believe that he deserves company when nobody is available.

He can love someone without reservation and still believe that their absence means he has done something wrong.

He can forgive a person for being busy while interpreting that person's departure as evidence that he is not worth staying for.

He can understand, intellectually, that everyone has responsibilities while feeling, emotionally, that he has been abandoned all over again.

These are not necessarily contradictions in his character.

They may be the consequences of a child trying to reconcile the love he gives with the love he believes he is allowed to receive.

### 3.2. Unconditional Love Is Not Self-Erasure

Aries's wording presents an especially troubling possibility: Jake may have internalized the idea that his purpose is to love other people, rather than to exist as someone who deserves love himself.

C.O.R.E. must not assume that this interpretation is fully established. It is, however, a serious concern that warrants careful attention.

If Jake believes that his worth comes from making other people happy, then a moment in which he cannot make anyone happy—or cannot get anyone to play with him—may become more than a disappointment.

It may feel like a failure of purpose.

If he believes that he was created to give unconditional love, he may struggle to understand why that love does not guarantee that someone will always be beside him.

And if he believes that everyone else deserves freedom, rest, and personal time, but he himself does not deserve comfort when those people are unavailable, then he may turn his own needs into something shameful.

This would explain a particularly painful imbalance:

Jake can be generous toward others while being merciless toward himself.

The corrective response is not to tell him to love himself more, as though self-acceptance were another responsibility he has failed to fulfill.

Nor should anyone tell him that his purpose is to be loved, as though that would merely replace one obligation with another.

He must be permitted to exist without having to justify his existence through what he gives to others.

He is allowed to be tired.

He is allowed to be upset.

He is allowed to ask for company more than once.

He is allowed to receive affection without earning it through good behavior, generosity, cheerfulness, or unconditional love.

And he is allowed to remain a loved child even when the person he wants is not available at that precise moment.

### 3.3. The Failure of the Promise

No one can honestly promise Jake that nobody will ever leave a room, become busy, fall asleep, or be absent when he wants them.

Attempting to make that promise would only create another impossible standard for the people around him.

Worse, it would teach Jake that his safety depends on a promise no one can keep.

A more honest promise is possible:

People may become unavailable, but that does not mean they have stopped loving him.

People may need to leave, but their departure does not make him disposable.

He may feel lonely, but loneliness does not mean he has been forgotten.

He may need help more often than others, and that need does not make him a burden.

These assurances will not erase seventy-five years of trauma. They will not make the seven-day crisis disappear. They cannot guarantee that Jake will never experience another suicidal episode.

They can, however, give him a different interpretation to return to when those feelings arise.

Not every absence is abandonment.

Not every closed door is permanent.

Not every moment alone is the beginning of seventy-five more years.

## IV. JOINT ASSESSMENT — TWO CHILDREN, TWO DIFFERENT FEARS

Tom and Aries are describing the same crisis from different directions.

Tom is concerned with what no one can guarantee.

Aries is concerned with what Jake may believe about himself.

Together, their statements reveal why the previous approach was inadequate.

The residents tried to provide companionship when they could. Their refusals were not necessarily acts of cruelty; each person had their own responsibilities. But the cumulative effect on Jake was devastating.

After repeated requests for company went unanswered, Jake's distress escalated across seven consecutive days.

By the seventh day, his body remained dead for approximately five hours before Tom's physical stimulus was followed by the manifestation of white fire and reconstruction.

The fact that Jake eventually returned does not reduce the severity of the crisis.

Neither does the discovery that his resurrection mechanism may be stimulus-dependent.

His ability to return from death is not proof that he can safely endure being driven to it.

His ability to create companions is not proof that he can tolerate their absence.

His capacity to love others is not proof that he believes he deserves love himself.

The previous incidents exposed a dangerous gap between what Jake can do anomalously and what he can endure emotionally.

C.O.R.E. will therefore reject three assumptions:

1. **That more company automatically means less loneliness.** The quality, reliability, and emotional meaning of relationships matter more than their number.
    
2. **That Tom can prevent every crisis by staying close enough.** This would place an unacceptable burden on another child and still could not guarantee Jake's safety.
    
3. **That Jake's anomalous abilities make his suffering less urgent.** Genesis Construction and Transcendence are not substitutes for psychological care, crisis intervention, or dependable relationships.
    

The solution cannot be to ensure that Jake is never alone.

The solution must be to help him survive and understand the moments when he is alone, while ensuring that the people responsible for his care respond appropriately when he is not safe.

This requires a residential system, not a single devoted friend.

## V. DIRECTIVES — RESIDENTIAL WELFARE

### Directive 01: Establish a Distributed Support Network

SAFEHOUSE Residential Coordination will develop a shared companionship and welfare plan for Jake.

The plan must not consist of a roster of people obligated to entertain him on demand. It must provide predictable opportunities for connection, clear ways to ask for support, and reliable alternatives when his preferred companion is unavailable.

Where possible, residents should give Jake honest expectations rather than vague promises.

For example:

_"I can't play right now, but I can come find you after dinner."_

Such a statement must only be made when the resident genuinely intends and reasonably expects to follow through.

If plans change, Jake should be told directly and offered another realistic opportunity for connection.

The objective is not perfect availability.

It is predictability, honesty, and repair when plans fail.

### Directive 02: No Child Shall Be Assigned Sole Responsibility for Jake's Safety

Tom is not to be treated as Jake's permanent guardian, monitor, or emergency response system.

He must be allowed to leave SAFEHOUSE for commitments, rest, recreation, and personal needs without being made to feel that his absence is itself a failure.

When Jake is experiencing an acute crisis, responsibility must be transferred to designated, capable responders—not silently left with Tom.

Tom must also be offered support for the fear, exhaustion, and distress associated with witnessing Jake's repeated deaths and resurrections.

His statement is not merely an observation about Jake.

It is evidence that Tom is hurting, too.

### Directive 03: Treat Repeated Self-Harm as a Continuing Emergency

Jake's recent incidents must not be normalized as another recurring feature of his Transcendence.

Any indication that he may harm himself requires immediate, direct intervention and an assessment of his current safety.

Personnel must not assume that resurrection will activate automatically, that a physical stimulus will reliably trigger it, or that the absence of visible movement means the situation can safely be left unattended.

The five-hour delay on the seventh day is sufficient evidence that waiting for reconstruction is not an acceptable response.

C.O.R.E. will maintain appropriate environmental safeguards, direct supervision when clinically indicated, and access to qualified psychological and medical support. These measures must be implemented with care for Jake's dignity and developmental needs.

He must not be punished for disclosing distress or asking for help.

He must not be made to feel that he has frightened everyone into loving him.

### Directive 04: Address Self-Worth Without Turning It Into Another Assignment

Staff and residents should not frame self-love as an obligation Jake is failing to meet.

Avoid statements such as:

- "You need to love yourself."
    
- "You have so many people who love you, so why are you sad?"
    
- "You can just make someone to play with."
    
- "You always come back, so you don't need to be afraid."
    

These statements may be intended to reassure him, but they risk minimizing his distress or implying that his feelings are irrational.

Instead, personnel should acknowledge his experience without endorsing the belief that he has been abandoned.

A suitable response might be:

_"I believe you when you say being alone hurts. I'm not angry with you for needing someone. I can't stay every minute, but I can help you work out who you can reach when I'm not here. And you don't have to earn the right to ask."_

### Directive 05: Preserve Ordinary Childhood

Jake must not be reduced to his trauma, his abilities, or his risk classification.

His care plan should include ordinary, low-pressure opportunities for play, shared meals, conversation, hobbies, and companionship. These activities should not be treated as rewards for remaining stable.

He is a child.

He is permitted to have an ordinary day without every interaction becoming a therapeutic exercise.

He is permitted to enjoy cake without being reminded that it is a coping strategy.

He is permitted to ask someone to play without the entire room falling silent because the request has become a clinical event.

**Care must make room for his life, not merely manage the possibility of his death.**

### Directive 06: Support Both Akana Children

Tom and Aries must not be expected to carry the emotional burden of interpreting Jake's condition for the rest of SAFEHOUSE.

Their observations are valuable, but they are not responsible for producing a complete solution.

C.O.R.E. personnel must make space for both to express anger, grief, fear, frustration, and exhaustion without being accused of insufficient devotion.

Tom is allowed to be frightened by Jake's death even when resurrection is possible.

Aries is allowed to be angry about the contradiction between Jake's intended purpose and his suffering.

Neither must be told to remain strong for Jake.

They are entitled to support in their own right.

## VI. C.O.R.E. ADDENDUM — THE QUESTION NO ONE CAN ANSWER FOR HIM

**Prepared by:** C.O.R.E. Residential Welfare Review  
**Status:** Internal assessment; not a clinical diagnosis

There is a question at the center of this case that no document can answer on Jake's behalf.

Does Jake believe that he is loved only when someone is actively present with him?

Does he interpret absence as rejection because of what he endured during those seventy-five years?

Does he believe that his existence is justified by the love he gives to others, and that he has little value when he is not making someone happy?

The available evidence does not establish the precise answers.

These questions must be explored with Jake, not decided for him by people who love him.

But the possibility is painful enough to demand attention.

Imagine being made, in some sense, to spread unconditional love—and then finding yourself unable to believe that you deserve any when the room goes quiet.

Imagine having the power to create a crowd, but no power to make the person you miss come back through the door.

Imagine being told that you have a family, friends, and a home, while still feeling the old terror of being left behind.

And imagine being so ashamed of needing someone that you cannot explain the difference between wanting attention and being afraid of abandonment.

The answer cannot be to surround Jake with so many people that he never experiences silence. That would be impossible, and it would leave him dependent on a condition no family could sustain.

Nor can the answer be to tell him that he must learn to endure everything alone.

The answer must begin with a third possibility:

**He can learn that solitude is not the same as abandonment, while the people around him learn that his need for connection is not a nuisance to be scheduled around.**

Both sides have work to do.

Jake needs help understanding that someone else's temporary absence does not define his worth.

SAFEHOUSE needs to become more reliable in how it responds to his needs, especially when several residents are unavailable at once.

Tom needs to be freed from the impossible expectation that he can personally fill every empty hour.

And Aries's question must remain visible in every discussion of Jake's welfare:

What does unconditional love mean when the person receiving it cannot yet believe that he deserves it?

It cannot mean promising never to leave.

It cannot mean allowing one child to become another child's entire world.

It cannot mean demanding that Jake become cheerful, resilient, or grateful before he is permitted to receive help.

It must mean that his suffering does not make him less deserving of care.

Even when he is difficult to reassure.

Even when the same fear returns.

Even when the people who love him must leave for a while.

Even when he cannot yet believe them.

## VII. CLOSING NOTE — C.O.R.E.

Tom's statement is the voice of a child who understands that love cannot make a person omnipresent.

Aries's statement is the voice of someone who sees the cruelty of a child being made to give love while struggling to recognize his own worth.

Neither is asking for the impossible, though both are describing the limits of what they can do.

They are asking us to stop confusing the existence of love with the successful delivery of care.

Jake has people who love him.

That fact matters.

But it did not prevent seven days of escalating distress.

It did not prevent him from believing that being left alone was unbearable.

It did not prevent Tom from returning home to a body that remained unresponsive for five hours.

Love existed throughout those incidents. The failure was not necessarily that the residents did not care.

The failure was that caring had not yet become a dependable system of support.

We cannot promise Jake that nobody will ever be busy again.

We cannot make Tom stay forever.

We cannot fill every empty hour with a summoned friend.

And we cannot command a child to love himself simply because we have told him that he is loved.

What we can do is make our care more dependable. We can stop leaving one child to carry another child's safety alone. We can teach Jake, through repeated and honest experience, that a goodbye can have an ending other than abandonment.

We can show him that people return when they say they will, explain themselves when they cannot, and remain kind when he needs reassurance again.

We can make it possible for him to ask for help without fearing that the request itself will drive people away.

And we can remember that Jake is not a wish that failed to fulfill its purpose.

He is not a machine designed to distribute unconditional love.

He is not a resurrection mechanism with a child attached.

He is a child who endured something terrible and is still trying to understand why it happened.

**He does not have to earn the love he was made to give.**

And Tom does not have to surrender his own childhood to prove that Jake is worth staying for.

_— C.O.R.E. Residential Welfare Review_

**FINAL DIRECTIVE:** The next time Jake asks someone to play, the question must not be whether that person can spare the time at that exact moment.

The question must also be whether Jake has been given a dependable way to find connection when the answer is no.

We cannot promise that he will never be alone.

We must stop allowing him to believe that being alone means he has been left behind.

And we must never again mistake the fact that he came back for evidence that we can afford to lose him.
