# SAFEHOUSE INTERNAL OBSERVATION

## JANE DOE'S ASSESSMENT OF NERI

**Observer:** Jane Doe  
**Subject:** Neri  
**Classification:** SAFEHOUSE INTERNAL  
**Purpose:** Behavioral and psychological understanding following repeated interaction

> **NOTE:** This is not a clinical diagnosis. Jane Doe is not a medical professional. The following represents an intelligence operative's behavioral assessment of a fellow resident after extended personal interaction.

---

## 1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

Jane Doe initially knew Neri as a quiet ten-year-old who ate cake, read chemistry books, and occasionally terrified people who made the mistake of bothering her friends.

That assessment was incomplete.

After spending significant time around Neri, Jane began recognizing a much larger pattern.

Neri is simultaneously:

- intensely protective of people she considers friends;
    
- emotionally defensive and easily embarrassed;
    
- extremely reluctant to ask for help;
    
- afraid that her existence is an inconvenience to others;
    
- deeply attached to symbols and routines associated with people she has lost;
    
- capable of severe emotional reactions when someone threatens those she loves;
    
- and, most importantly, **remarkably indifferent toward the possibility of her own death.**
    

Jane describes this combination informally as:

> **"She's a tsundere, she's a yandere, and somehow the scariest part is that she doesn't seem to think she's worth saving."**

---

# 2. THE TSUNDERE COMPONENT

Neri's defensive personality is relatively easy to identify once the pattern is understood.

She dislikes overt affection.

She dislikes being called cute.

She becomes embarrassed when someone is openly concerned about her.

She frequently responds to kindness with irritation, denial, or a short answer.

However, her behavior consistently contradicts her words.

If someone she cares about is hurt:

She cares.

If someone is frightened:

She notices.

If someone disappears:

She searches.

If someone threatens her friends:

She intervenes.

If someone gives her something meaningful:

She remembers it.

Jane's assessment:

> **"She'll tell you to leave her alone while quietly making sure you're okay."**

This is not merely shyness.

It appears to be a defensive habit developed over a long period of time.

Neri is much more comfortable **doing something for someone** than admitting she needs something herself.

---

# 3. THE YANDERE COMPONENT

Jane's classification here is informal.

Neri is not indiscriminately violent.

She does not hurt people simply because she dislikes them.

Her behavior follows a much more specific rule:

> **Friend → protect.**  
> **Threat → confront.**  
> **Friend injured → extreme protective response.**

Her attachment to friends can become extraordinarily intense.

Jane observed that Neri's greatest fear isn't necessarily being hurt herself.

It is losing somebody else.

This distinction is critical.

Neri has repeatedly demonstrated willingness to endanger herself when protecting another person.

Jane's interpretation:

> **"She doesn't think, 'I might get hurt.'**
> 
> **"She thinks, 'They're getting hurt.'"**
> 
> **"That's the entire calculation."**

This explains why conventional deterrence is unreliable against her.

Threatening Neri may make her defensive.

Threatening somebody she loves can make her reckless.

---

# 4. WHY NERI IS AFRAID OF BOTHERING PEOPLE

This was one of the more difficult pieces for Jane to understand.

Neri doesn't merely dislike asking for help.

She appears to believe that **requiring help makes her a burden.**

The distinction is important.

When Neri experiences symptoms, her first instinct is often not:

> _"I need medical attention."_

It is:

> _"I'll wait."_

If someone is busy:

> _"I shouldn't interrupt them."_

If someone is already helping another person:

> _"They have something more important to do."_

If she is hurt:

> _"It'll go away."_

Jane eventually recognized the underlying belief:

**Neri places other people's needs above her own by default.**

This is particularly dangerous because Neri's medical condition does not respect that philosophy.

Anemia does not care whether she wants to bother someone.

Her cardiovascular system does not care whether the cafeteria is busy.

Her blood does not care whether everyone else has work to do.

Jane summarized the problem bluntly:

> **"Her body can kill her while she's politely waiting for everyone to finish their shift."**

---

# 5. WHY SHE LIKES CAKE

Jane originally assumed Neri simply had an unusually strong sweet tooth.

That explanation proved insufficient.

The truth is considerably more personal.

Neri's younger sister loved sweets.

Cake, candy, pastries and similar foods became associated with her sister.

Neri's relationship with sweets therefore developed from grief.

She eats cake partly because it reminds her of someone she loved.

There is another layer to it.

Neri's sister was treated as disposable by their family.

Neri couldn't protect her.

She remembers that.

Consequently, eating something her sister loved became a way of keeping a small piece of her sister present.

Jane's assessment:

> **"The cake isn't just cake."**

Neri does genuinely enjoy sweets now.

She isn't necessarily crying every time she eats cake.

Most of the time, she simply enjoys it.

But the original attachment remains.

This also explains why Neri can become unusually cooperative and comfortable around people who share sweets with her.

Cake represents something much safer to her than food.

It represents **someone she loved being remembered.**

---

# 6. THE CHILDHOOD

Jane eventually pieced together the broad history.

Neri was artificially created/transformed and was originally designated **002** rather than given a normal name.

Her younger sister called her **Neri**.

That name remained.

Her family treated children as useful assets rather than people.

Neri was subjected to severe abuse, neglect, experimentation, and physical harm.

Her younger sister was the person she loved most.

Her sister loved sweets.

Her family considered her sister disposable.

Neri witnessed her sister being killed and was unable to stop it.

She was also highly capable in chemistry.

Her family dismissed it as useless.

Later, Neri formed friendships.

Several of those friends died by self-harm not long after she became close to them.

To a healthy adult, these events would be understood as circumstances beyond a child's control.

Neri did not process them that way.

She internalized them.

The pattern became:

> **"People I care about disappear."**

and eventually:

> **"I failed to protect them."**

That belief is one of the foundations of her current behavior.

---

# 7. WHY NERI FIGHTS SO HARD

Jane now understands that Neri's combat behavior isn't primarily about aggression.

It is about **prevention.**

Neri learned that people she loved could disappear permanently.

So when somebody threatens a friend, her brain doesn't necessarily interpret the situation as:

> _"There is a fight."_

It interprets it as:

> _"This is happening again."_

That makes her response disproportionately intense.

She isn't trying to prove that she's strong.

She is trying to make sure nobody disappears.

Jane's conclusion:

> **"She's not protecting people because she thinks she's powerful."**
> 
> **"She's protecting people because she knows what happens when nobody does."**

---

# 8. THE MOST CONCERNING DISCOVERY

Everything above was concerning.

None of it frightened Jane as much as one particular realization.

Neri does not appear to regard her own death as particularly serious.

Not in the dramatic sense.

Not because she wants to die.

Not because she is actively seeking death.

Something more subtle is happening.

She simply doesn't seem to assign **herself** the same value she assigns to everyone else.

When Jane asked what would have happened during her latest anemic episode if Bao had not arrived, Neri answered:

> **"I would've died."**

Then she went back to her cake.

There was no panic.

No horror.

No:

> _"I don't want to die."_

No:

> _"I'm glad Bao was there."_

Just acceptance.

Jane recognized the pattern immediately.

Neri doesn't necessarily want to die.

She simply doesn't seem particularly surprised by the possibility.

---

# 9. NERI'S VIEW OF HER OWN DEATH

Jane's assessment is that Neri treats her own death as an **acceptable outcome** whenever her survival conflicts with somebody else's wellbeing.

This is extremely dangerous.

Consider the difference:

**Someone threatens Neri:**

> "I'll hurt you."

Neri can become defensive.

**Someone threatens her friend:**

> "I'll hurt them."

Neri may immediately intervene.

**Neri begins experiencing an anemic episode:**

> "Something's wrong."

Her response may be:

> "I'll wait."

The third situation is the one Jane finds most disturbing.

Neri will fight an external threat with everything she has.

But when the threat comes from inside her own body, she can simply sit there and wait for it to pass.

Even when the consequence can be death.

Jane's private conclusion:

> **"She knows how to fight for everybody except herself."**

---

# 10. THE CONTRADICTION

This creates an extraordinary contradiction.

Neri believes other people deserve protection.

She believes friends should not be abandoned.

She believes losing somebody is devastating.

She will cross enormous distances to find someone who disappears.

She will fight people much stronger than herself to protect someone.

Yet she can apparently accept her own death without the same urgency.

Jane believes this is partly because Neri learned a very early lesson:

**Other people are worth saving.**

She never learned the second half:

**So are you.**

---

# 11. JANE'S RESPONSE

Jane did not tell Neri:

> "You need to value yourself."

That would likely accomplish very little.

Instead, Jane began using simpler language.

When Neri says she doesn't want to bother someone:

> **"You're not bothering them."**

When Neri says she's fine despite obvious symptoms:

> **"You're not fine. Sit down."**

When Neri attempts to handle something dangerous alone:

> **"You have people now."**

When Neri worries about somebody else:

> **"We'll help them. You don't have to destroy yourself doing it."**

And when Neri treats another anemic episode like an inconvenience:

Jane becomes serious.

> **"No."**

Neri:

> **"No what?"**

> **"No pretending this is normal."**

---

# 12. THE CONVERSATION

One evening, Jane finally confronted the underlying issue.

> **Jane:** "Neri."

> **Neri:** "Hm?"

> **Jane:** "If you died tomorrow, would you think that was a big deal?"

Neri stopped eating.

She thought about it.

> **Neri:** "For everyone else."

Jane's expression changed.

> **Jane:** "And for you?"

Neri didn't answer.

Jane already had her answer.

> **Jane:** "That's what I was afraid of."

Neri looked confused.

> **Neri:** "Why?"

> **Jane:** "Because you're wrong."

Neri frowned.

> **Neri:** "About what?"

Jane pointed at her.

> **Jane:** "You."

Neri blinked.

> **Jane:** "You're not an expendable asset."

Silence.

> **Jane:** "You're not 002."

Neri's expression softened slightly.

> **Jane:** "You're not somebody who can just disappear and leave everyone else to deal with it."

Jane paused.

> **Jane:** "You're Neri."

Neri looked down.

Jane continued.

> **Jane:** "You're a daughter."

> **Jane:** "You're a friend."

> **Jane:** "You're someone's big sister."

> **Jane:** "You're someone's reason to bake another cake."

Neri's eyes began to water.

Jane didn't push further.

> **Jane:** "And you're allowed to stay."

Neri quietly wiped her eyes with her sleeve.

> **Neri:** "…Okay."

Jane nodded.

> **Jane:** "Good."

A pause.

> **Neri:** "Can I have another cake?"

Jane stared at her.

> **Jane:** "Yes."

Neri smiled.

> **Neri:** "Okay."

---

# 13. FINAL ASSESSMENT

Jane Doe's assessment of Neri changed substantially following these interactions.

Neri is not simply a dangerous child.

She is not simply traumatized.

She is not simply protective.

She is a child who learned extremely early that:

**Love means protecting people.**

**Losing people means failure.**

**Needing people means inconvenience.**

**Her own suffering is tolerable.**

And, most dangerously:

**Her own survival is optional.**

Jane considers the final point to be Neri's greatest vulnerability.

Not because Neri wants to die.

She doesn't.

Rather, because she may fail to recognize that she is **supposed to prevent it.**

Jane's final handwritten note attached to the assessment reads:

> **"I spent five years learning how to get information out of people."**
> 
> **"Neri didn't need extracting."**
> 
> **"She just needed somebody to actually listen."**
> 
> **"And now I know what she needs most."**
> 
> **"Someone to tell her that her life counts, too."**

**END OF ASSESSMENT**
