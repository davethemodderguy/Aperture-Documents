# **APERTURE INDUSTRIES**

## **C.O.R.E. – CENTRAL OPERATIONS & RESEARCH EXECUTIVE**

### **MEDICAL BEHAVIORAL OBSERVATION**

## **SUBJECT: CORIN LOCKWOOD – ROUTINE MEDICAL EXAMINATIONS**

**Classification:** LEVEL 7 – Medical Personnel Only

---

# **OVERVIEW**

Routine medical examinations remain one of Corin Lockwood's least preferred activities.

Although she has never refused a scheduled examination, Medical Services consistently observe heightened anxiety before and during every appointment.

Corin is cooperative throughout the procedure.

She is also visibly uncomfortable.

---

# **BEHAVIOR DURING EXAMINATIONS**

Upon arriving at Medical Services, Corin typically becomes noticeably quieter than usual.

She answers questions politely.

She avoids eye contact.

She keeps her hands folded in her lap while waiting.

Personnel familiar with her recognize these behaviors as signs of anxiety rather than unwillingness to cooperate.

When called into the examination room, Corin has been observed quietly apologizing before the examination has even begun.

No one has determined why.

---

# **DIAGNOSTIC EQUIPMENT**

Corin's Ether-dependent physiology differs substantially from ordinary human biology.

As a result, conventional medical equipment frequently reports findings that would normally be associated with death or equipment failure.

Depending on the device used, common observations include:

- No detectable heartbeat.
    
- No measurable peripheral blood circulation using standard techniques.
    
- Skin temperature far below ordinary human ranges.
    
- Minimal or absent thermal signature.
    

Medical personnel trained in Corin's condition recognize these findings as expected for her unique physiology.

However, the equipment itself does not distinguish between "unexpected physiology" and "no signs of life."

---

# **FIRST-TIME STAFF REACTIONS**

Despite advance briefings, newly assigned medical personnel occasionally react with surprise when presented with Corin's readings.

Several have instinctively rechecked equipment calibration.

Others have assumed a sensor malfunction.

One resident physician reportedly looked from the monitor to Corin, then back to the monitor three separate times before quietly asking:

> "...You're feeling okay?"

Corin answered with an embarrassed smile.

> "...I'm okay."

Medical Services later noted that Corin appeared significantly more withdrawn for the remainder of the examination.

---

# **CORIN'S PERSPECTIVE**

During a follow-up consultation, Corin was asked why routine examinations upset her so much.

She hesitated before answering.

> "...The machines aren't wrong."

She looked down.

> "...They just remind me."

After a long silence, she continued.

> "...Every appointment..."

> "...I watch another monitor tell me I shouldn't be here."

She smiled weakly.

It was not a happy smile.

> "...It's embarrassing."

> "...And it's scary."

---

# **LUMI'S OBSERVATION**

Lumi has accompanied Corin to several appointments.

She noticed that Corin never seemed afraid of needles.

Or blood tests.

Or scans.

Instead, Corin watched the monitors.

As though she already knew what they would say.

After one examination, Lumi quietly asked:

> "Do those screens frighten you?"

Corin nodded.

> "...Not because of what they say."

Another pause.

> "...Because they remind me of why they say it."

Lumi gently took her daughter's hand.

Neither spoke for the rest of the walk home.

---

# **MEDICAL RECOMMENDATIONS**

Medical Services recommend that personnel explain diagnostic findings to newly assigned staff before Corin enters the room, reducing unnecessary surprise and preventing avoidable distress.

Whenever possible, clinicians should focus discussions on Corin's current health and well-being rather than emphasizing the unusual nature of her physiology.

The objective of a routine examination is to care for the patient.

Not to remind her of the trauma that altered her life.

---

# **CURRENT STATUS**

Corin continues to attend all scheduled medical examinations.

She remains cooperative and respectful toward staff.

Nevertheless, routine checkups continue to be emotionally difficult, as the equipment often echoes the same message that Corin has struggled against for years.

Project SAFEHOUSE residents therefore make a point of greeting her warmly after each appointment.

Not because the examination went badly.

But because they want the last thing Corin remembers that day to be people who are happy to see her—not the monitors.

**End of Medical Behavioral Observation**
